How did these kuashang of different occupations and in different sectors interact with each other, and how fluid is the concept of kuashang? How the notion of kuashang differed from huashang could also be further elaborated. While huashang clearly refers to overseas Chinese merchants who linked overseas Chinese with China, hua also carries the connotation of “ethnically Chinese.” Assuming that many of these huashang had been trading across borders for centuries and many of the kuashang remained ethnically Chinese, how different were the two concepts? In addition, when discussing informal decolonization, the author could provide a more powerful argument by further explaining the relationship between decolonization and American imperialism. As the chapters have demonstrated, the educational institutions and industrial market were gradually departing from the system of the British empire. However, it might be worth considering if shifting towards a system of American imperialism genuinely constituted “decolonization.” In the educational chapters, it would also be useful to examine the educational curriculum and syllabus adopted in the US-oriented schools, which would further strengthen the claim on “informal decolonization.” Made in Hong Kong provides a foundation to view Hong Kong history from a transnational perspective. It will be of interest to audiences within and beyond academia, especially scholars who work on the economic and social history of China, Hong Kong and colonialism.
