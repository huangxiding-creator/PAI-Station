Abstract
The document proposes three concepts—a globalization process with Chinese 
characteristics, new triangular relationships and security shoring—to discuss the 
maturity, extension and depth of the socioeconomic relationship between Mexico 
and China. These concepts may also provide valuable insights for future studies. 
Drawing on these concepts, the analysis focuses on bilateral institutions and trade, 
Chinese overseas foreign direct investments (OFDI) in Mexico and infrastructure 
projects. These examinations allow for detailed understanding of China’s increas-
ing presence in Mexico, as well as its challenges. In the case of bilateral trade, for 
example, Mexican imports from China have increased significantly throughout the 
twenty-first century and substituting for US imports; the US is however by far still 
the most important trading partner of Mexico. The systemic competition and con-
frontation between the US and China are generating massive challenges in third 
countries and regions such as Latin America and the Caribbean and Mexico. The 
chapter concludes with a summary of key findings and insights, followed by a series 
of proposals.

2  Concepts needed to understand the China‑Mexico socioeconomic 
relationship
Drawing on almost three decades of socioeconomic analysis of the China-LAC and 
China-Mexico relationships, at least three concepts are required to fully comprehend 
the conditions and challenges of the contemporary bilateral socioeconomic relation-
ship between Mexico and China.1
First, the proposal for a “process of globalization with Chinese characteristics” 
that has been put forth since 2013, emphasizing “interconnectivity” and infrastruc-
ture projects as fundamental elements of international cooperation. In contrast to 
the globalization process that emerged since Bretton Woods after World War II—
characterized by the liberalization of goods, services, and capital, benefitting a 1 
percent/10 percent of the global population (Rodrik 2011; Stiglitz 2016) and led 
by the United States, the European Union and Japan—China’s proposal focuses on 
improving the lives of those in the Global South. By emphasizing “interconnectiv -
ity”, specifically infrastructure projects, this initiative aims to benefit the remaining 
99 to 90 percent of the global population. Thus, the contemporary proposal for a 
globalization process with Chinese characteristics is a direct and explicit alterna-
tive to the globalization process led by the US and sets the bases for a contemporary 
confrontation. The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) since 2013 could be understood 
as an umbrella institution for China’s cooperation with the Global South and inte-
grating hundreds of concrete cooperation schemes globally and with specific regions

Second, and since more than a decade (Dussel Peters et  al. 2013) a group of 
authors have insisted the concept of “new triangular relationships”. Systemic com-
petition2—characterized by contrasting economic and political systems since the 
second decade of the twenty-first century—refers to the global historical possibility, 
in contrast to the process since the 1940 s, that third regions and countries engage 
into strategic socioeconomic relations in the short, medium and long-run with the 
United States and with the People’s Republic of China. This engagement occurs 
pragmatically across hundreds of issues, including 6G, electric vehicles, Confucious 
Institutes, and tourism. The concept is relevant from several perspectives. On the 
one hand, it allows for a historical analysis to comprehend the details of the systemic 
competition among the two largest global economies: in culture, tourism, academics, 
technology, trade, investments, infrastructure projects and others, i.e. it is not only a 
matter of a “trade war” and tariffs, a fashionable item (Bown 2025). Additionally, 
the concept highlights that third regions and countries—for example for LAC and 
Mexico—will face significant challenges in the future regarding the United States 
and China (and not engaging in debate on the US or China). The topic is critical 
to establish strategic agendas—for example regarding Brazil (Rosito 2020) or Mex-
ico (Arsovska et al. 2024)—that establish the amplitude and depth of their relations 
with the US and China, acknowledging important differences within regions such 
as in LAC (ECLAC 2025). The concept of “new triangular relationships” distances 
itself from the concepts of (new) Cold Wars (Green 2025; Wang 2023) which refers 
to global structures from the middle of the twentieth century and with little resem-
blance to the third decade of the third decade of the twenty-first century. The Soviet 
Union of the twentieth century has no relationship to China in the twenty-first cen-
tury. China is currently investing 2.5% of its GDP in R&D, with an absolute amount 
similar to that of the US. The US–China Economic and Security Review Commis-
sion of the Congress (2024) highlights its preoccupation on China’s technological 
achievements, particularly in advanced semiconductors, energy, computation, cloud 
computing, artificial intelligence (AI) and access to data for AI, as well as quan-
tic technology, biotechnology, and advanced batteries. In several areas of innova-
tion China has already achieved impressive results (ASPI 2024): in 2003–2007 the 
US accounted for 60 of 64 core technologies (China only 3), in 2020–2024 China 
became the leader in 57 of 64 “core technologies”.3

3.1  Background: Mexico’s development strategy since 1988 and Mexico–China 
bilateral institutions
To discuss details of the contemporary socioeconomic relationship between Mexico 
and China, two background issues are relevant. First, Mexico’s economic devel-
opment in the last decades and, second, the institutional setting of the bilateral 
relationship.
There is an important and in-depth analysis on Mexico’s socioeconomy since the 
1980 s from a macro, meso, micro and territorial perspective (De la Garza 1989; 
Dussel Peters 2000; Gereffi 2025; Moreno-Brid and Ros 2009). While Mexico’s 
quick and profound strategy of export-oriented industrialization (EOI) implemented 
by President Salinas de Gortari since 1988 generated proved helpful in controlling 
inflation, the fiscal deficit, and particularly in achieving significant growth in foreign 
trade (as we shall see below in detail), performance in other socioeconomic areas 
was disappointing. Economic growth measured in GDP was very low compared to 
Mexico’s historical dynamism, the investment coefficient in terms of GDP did not 
increase, and real minimum wages were far below levels of the early 1980s. Several 
of the promises of EOI were simply not accomplished (ECLAC 2018).
From an industrial organization perspective Mexico was characterized by a pro-
found and general socioeconomic polarization (at the level of firms, sectors, and ter-
ritories) as a result of an export-orientation of a very small group of foreign and 
Mexican firms with minimal backward and forward linkages. Historical incentives 
for exports -through exemptions in tariffs, income and value-added taxes- resulted 
in disincentives in time (or the lack of a process of “territorial endogeneity” (Dussel 
Peters 2000). As a result, and until 2025, Mexico’s export processes specialize in 
processes and goods of low value-added (“temporary imports to be exported”).8 The 
differences in productivity growth and growth in real wages (the “Fordist equation”) 
have increased particularly for export-oriented firms and sectors, and this gap gener-
ates generalized tensions and challenges for the macro, meso, micro, and territorial 
stability of the Mexican economy.

Multiple structural shortcomings in Mexico’s economy—including sluggish 
growth, limited fiscal capacity and particularly in terms of its income, minimal 
investments in research and development, a weak domestic supplier system in its 
export-orientation, and low real minimum and manufacturing wages—arise from its 
industrial organization. The export-oriented processes and products manufactured in 
Mexico depend heavily on temporary imports for export.
The Mexico-China bilateral relationship was defined as a “Strategic Association” 
in 2003 and was elevated in 2013 as an “Integral Strategic Association”; the creation 
of the Permanent Binational Commission in 2004, the High-Level Group (or GAN, 
in 2004), the High Level Group in Business (GANE, 2013), and the High-Level 
Group in Investments (GANI, 2014) all reflect important efforts by both countries 
to engage on specific relevant issues. These bilateral institutions exist in addition to 
regional efforts such as the CELAC (Community of Latin American and Caribbean 
States)-China Forum since 2015 (Mexico held the pro tempore presidency in 2021 
and 2022). Recognizing this rich institutional fabric, it is equally important to note 
to highlight that these institutions have not been able to monitor and evaluate the 
respective periodic meetings for nearly two decades. While maintaining a formal 
dialogue is important, these discussions have not effectively addressed contempo-
rary bilateral issues. For instance, topics such as China’s recognition as a market 
economy in the framework of the World Trade Organization since 2001 or China’s 
proposal to access to CPTPP (Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-
Pacific Partnership) since September of 2021 have not been properly addressed. 
Recent unilateral proposals to increase Chinese imports have not been discussed in 
any of these bilateral institutions so far.
Until the midst of 2025 Mexico has not been able to provide for a strategic agenda 
in the multiple fields of the bilateral relationship with China. This rather surpris-
ing condition, and in the context of the massive socioeconomic presence of China 
in Mexico (see next chapter), also present in most of Latin America and the Car -
ibbean, is a result of several factors. On the one hand, understanding that public, 
private, academic and other institutions in Mexico specializing on China are weak 
and, with exceptions (Arsovska et al. 2024); they do not provide periodic analysis, 
assessments, and proposals. Conversely, as discussed above, security shoring is sig-
nificantly impacting Mexico, and given that the US is still by far the most important 
foreign country for Mexico, this influence warrants further exploration down below. 
These weaknesses are also reflected in the bilateral China-Mexico institutions. As a 
result, and until 2025, these domestic institutions are not strong enough to push for a 
national strategy and agenda vis a vis China in the public and legislatives.

3.2.1  Trade
This has been one of the most analyzed topics in the Mexico–China relationship 
(ECLAC 2025; Dussel Peters 2025a; Liu Sun 2024, 197–219). Mexico has been 
incredibly successful in integrating into US trade, becoming the US’ main trading 
partner since 2023 and displacing other trading partners such as China and Can-
ada. While it is true that Mexico has increased its share over total US trade—from 
12.33% in 2010 to 15.76% in 2024 (Fig.  1)—both Canada’s and China’s share have 
fallen dramatically. This is particularly the case for China’s trade with the US, 
which fell from 16.34% in 2017—and being the US’ main trading partner during 
2015–2018—to 10.92% in 2024. The profound US–China confrontation since the 
first Trump presidency and security shoring have had profound bilateral implica-
tions on trade.9
China has become the most dynamic country in Mexico’s foreign trade in the 
twenty-first century and is its second trading partner since 2003 (Fig.  2). This pro-
cess has been dramatic since the 1990s: in the early 1990 s the share of China in 
Mexico’s foreign trade accounted for less than 1%; it increased to 11.25% in 2024. 
The main source of growth of bilateral trade has been Mexican imports from China, 
which represented 20.76% of Mexican imports in 2024. The US, on the other side, 
has seen a big decline in its share of Mexico’s foreign trade, which decreased 81.03% 
in 1999 to 61.45% in 2024. This downward trend has persisted throughout the years,

4  Conclusions and recommendations
The first section focuses on a group of concepts that are important for the analysis of 
the LAC and Mexican socioeconomic relationship in the twenty-first century. This 
is particularly the case for Mexico, which, like no other country in LAC, shares a 
3100 km border and serves as its most relevant socioeconomic, political and cultural 
point of reference. Nevertheless, in light of the growing Chinese presence in Mex-
ico, the concept of new triangular relationships is essential. In light of the US-China 
systemic competition, third regions and countries will have to define specific agen-
das on hundreds of issues regarding the US and China. Since the implementation of 
security shoring policies by the U.S. in 2022, third countries, particularly Mexico, 
are facing significant challenges, as discussed in detail in the case for Mexico.
Like no other countries in LAC, Mexico has specialized since the late 1980 s on 
processes to import parts and components to be exported, mainly to the US; these 
processes have continued until today. As presented in the second chapter, this trade 
structure has significant impacts on its development model, as well as on macro, 
meso, micro, and territorial structures; China’s recent and increasing socioeconomic 
presence in Mexico is modifying these structures. This new triangular relationship 
and security shoring since 2022 is generating substantial uncertainty in Mexico and 
other countries in LAC. The second chapter of the analysis examines in detail the 
main features of the bilateral socioeconomic Mexico-China relationship.
It is important to acknowledge an important group of bilateral institutions since 
2004. Surprisingly, Mexican institutions committed to China are limited and have, 
so far, not allowed for establishing a short, medium and long-term agenda with 
China.
However, the second chapter allows for a detailed understanding of the matu-
rity, depth and extension of the socioeconomic relationship between Mexico and 
China. In terms of trade, probably the item with the most significant Chinese pres-
ence today, Mexican imports from China accounted in 2024 for the first time for 
more than 20% of total imports, while the US share has been falling continuously 
since the end of the 1990s. The concept of new triangular relationships is critical:

throughout the twenty-first century, Mexican imports from China have been rising, 
along with the Chinese value-added in Mexican exports to the U.S. In other words, 
Mexican imports have increasingly replaced U.S. goods with those from China. 
At the same time, it is mainly foreign and US-firms that import Chinese goods in 
Mexico. In the field of investments China has also increased its presence, but only 
accounting for an accumulated OFDI in Mexico of 3.36% over total foreign invest-
ments during 2000–2024, i.e. 96.64% of Mexico’s foreign investments are non-Chi-
nese. As discussed in the chapter, Chinese OFDI in Mexico is highly concentrated in 
Chinese private firms and in Mexico’s manufacturing (and in autoparts and automo-
biles), contrary to LAC. Finally, Chinese infrastructure projects in Mexico have not 
only been extremely dynamic since 2017, and compared to other LAC countries, but 
also in their specialization in transportation and far less in energy; in the latter 100% 
of Chinese infrastructure projects concentrated in renewable projects. In Mexico 
the level of firm-level concentration in Chinese infrastructure projects, both in the 
amount and the generated employment, is much higher than for LAC.
A group of precise policy proposals for Mexico vis a vis China have been pre-
sented recently (Arsovska et  al. 2024) with an explicit agenda for Mexico’s trade 
structure and integrating business, the Senate and other responsible public, private 
and academic sectors. The basis for such a short, medium and long-term agenda. 
However, requires a change in Mexico’s economic and political elites to concentrate 
on shifting socioeconomic structures in the twenty-first century in its relationship 
with the US and China. This process has not taken place yet and, as a result, Mexico 
has been able to keep on postponing an extensive and detailed agenda towards China 
until today. So far most of Mexican (and LAC) elites have fallen into the trap of a 
discussion on “either the US or China” and not comprehending China’s profound 
integration to Mexico’s productive structure. Very recent proposals, in September 
2025, might indicate that the government is quickly shifting to an “only US” sce-
nario through massive high tariffs on Chinese imports; such policies, however, do 
not understand the new triangular relationship that has evolved in Mexico during the 
twenty-first century given massive Chinese imports mainly pursued by foreign firms 
and particularly from the United States.
Only with the acknowledgement of these shifting socioeconomic structures in the 
twenty-first century, Mexico would be able to examine details in trade, investments, 
and infrastructure projects with China. The existing bilateral institutions, as well as 
the “integral strategic association” since 2013 require a modernization and evalu-
ation of the negotiated topics since the early twenty-first century. It is in this light 
that trade, OFDI, infrastructure projects, as well as dozens of other important bilat-
eral items (such as tourism, education, academics, telecommunications, culture, etc.) 
could be updated.
