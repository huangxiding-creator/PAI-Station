Abstract 
Chinese engineering enterprises have taken on an increasing number of overseas infrastructure 
projects as the Belt and Road Initiative has progressed, and the industry now looks for engineering 
management graduates who combine solid theoretical kn owledge with practical skills such as contract 
interpretation, risk identification, cross -cultural performance, and dispute resolution. The traditional 
International Engineering Management course, built on lecture -based instruction and simplified cases, 
falls short of real overseas performance scenarios. PBL (problem -based learning) uses genuine 
engineering conflicts as learning vehicles and relies on group collaboration to complete material 
review, contract analysis, solution development, and report writin g, effectively bridging classroom 
theory and overseas engineering practice. Before the course began, this study distributed a 
questionnaire to enrolled students and collected 85 valid responses. Combining the authors’ own 
teaching experience, it selected t he Padma Bridge Highway Link EPC Project in Bangladesh as a 
research case and, based on the FIDIC 2017 Silver Book Conditions of Contract, designed six 
engineering dispute topics for PBL teaching practice. The paper describes the whole teaching process, 
reports the reform effects, and offers suggestions for the problems identified, which may be useful for 
international engineering course construction in Chinese universities. 
Keywords 
International engineering management, PBL teaching, FIDIC 2017 Silver  Book, EPC turnkey 
contracting, The Belt and Road Initiative

1. Introduction 
1.1 Research Background 
Over the past decade or so of Belt and Road cooperation, Chinese enterprises have greatly expanded 
their overseas infrastructure contracting, covering num erous countries in Asia, Africa, and Europe, and 
the EPC turnkey model has become the dominant form of international project delivery. Because the 
legal systems of the host countries vary, multiple risks —exchange rate fluctuations, local labor 
regulations, extreme climatic disasters, changes in employer instructions, and cross -cultural 
communication conflicts—are intertwined, and engineering managers therefore need strong abilities in 
contract interpretation, risk anticipation, and dispute handling. 
For undergraduate engineering management majors, the International Engineering Management course 
deals with the international engineering contract system, risk management methods, cross -cultural 
management, and variation and claim handling. In classroom practice,  some classes still spend most of 
the time explaining FIDIC provisions and theoretical knowledge, and the cases used are mostly 
idealized simplifications without the messy conflicts of real project performance. Students remain 
largely passive receivers of knowledge and seldom analyze complex performance conflicts from the 
general contractor’s perspective. When they meet real overseas engineering scenarios, students often 
misjudge who bears the risk, apply contract clauses in the wrong way, or cannot build a  complete chain 
of claim logic. 
Unlike the traditional lecture -driven classroom, PBL starts from real and complex engineering 
problems. Students work in groups to consult contract documents, collect project background 
information, identify the risk boundar ies of each party, and produce solutions, which puts students in 
the center of the learning process and turns theoretical knowledge into practical ability. Bringing the 
real overseas benchmark projects of the Belt and Road Initiative into PBL teaching desi gn can raise the 
effectiveness of the course and align it better with the industry’s actual talent needs.  
1.2 Domestic and International Research Status 
PBL first appeared in medical education and was later brought into the engineering field. In medical 
education, PBL takes clinical cases as the starting point and is generally acknowledged as an effective 
model of medical teaching. In the field of international engineering management, PBL is used to let 
students enter complex real engineering problems, and the related research is still being deepened, with 
the application of this model to specific courses and the exploration of real cases remaining relatively 
scarce. In the practice of this course, the teaching model is carried out around the FIDIC contract,  
centering on real project cases, with risk allocation, claim procedures, and dispute resolution as an 
important part of scenario-based training. 
Domestic scholars have also studied the reform of the International Engineering Management course, 
mostly from the perspectives of FIDIC contract teaching, bilingual teaching, and project management 
teaching system construction. However, the reports on the whole -process teaching practice that

1.3 Research Content and Significance 
The work of this paper is organized around four tasks. It first sorts out the overall research status of the 
PBL teaching model in the field of international engineering management, then introduces the basic 
situation of the Padma project and its typical performance risks, then explains in detail the PBL 
teaching design and implementation process of the course, and finally reviews the teaching effects, 
identifies the remaining shortcomings, and puts forward corresponding improvement paths.  
Theoretically, the paper adds to the PBL teaching case resources of the course, refines the ideas of 
case-based teaching reform, and offers implementation plans that similar courses can refer to. 
Practically, training students with the complex scenarios of a real project helps them read FIDIC 
clauses, build international engineering risk awareness, and sharpen their abilities in contract 
interpretation, variati on and claim handling, and cross -cultural performance, so that the graduates 
cultivated are better aligned with the needs of overseas engineering positions.  
1.4 Questionnaire Survey and Analysis of the Current Teaching Situation 
To learn about the actual l earning conditions and needs of the students taking the course, and to keep 
the reform from being driven only by experience, the authors surveyed the enrolled students before the 
course started. The questionnaire covered students’ basic information, previo us course foundation, 
course selection motivation, career aspirations, attitude toward the PBL teaching model, acceptance of 
bilingual teaching, teaching method preferences, and ability training needs, and 85 valid questionnaires 
were collected. The survey results confirmed on the one hand that the traditional classroom needs to be 
reformed, and on the other hand gave direct evidence of the students’ learning situation for the 
subsequent teaching design. 
On their prior preparation, 51.76% of the respondents  had never taken any project management course, 
and only 23.53% had completed Engineering Project Management. When it comes to the PBL teaching 
method, 63.53% knew nothing about it, and only 1.18% had actually participated in similar learning. 
As for Engli sh proficiency, 76.47% had not yet passed CET -4. Taken together, students selecting this 
course generally lack prerequisite knowledge, know little about PBL, and have a weak English 
foundation, so the reform should first of all lower the entry threshold.

2. Theoretical Overview of the PBL Teaching Model 
2.1 The Connotation of the PBL Teaching Model 
PBL, the problem-based learning model, departs from the traditional logic of teaching theory first and 
then doing exercises. Its core feature  is to start from ill-structured problems that are complex and come 
from real engineering. Students learn in groups. In order to solve real engineering conflicts, they 
actively collect materials, study contract texts, and carry out group discussions, finally for ming 
solutions. Teachers mainly play the roles of guidance and comments and generally do not directly give 
standard answers in class. Most disputes arising in the international engineering performance process 
are ill-structured problems without a unique optimal solution, which matches the characteristics of PBL 
teaching very well. 
PBL teaching essentially involves the following core elements: engineering problems originating from 
reality, group collaborative inquiry, students’ autonomous learning, teachers’  guidance and comments, 
output presentation, and after-class review and reflection. 
2.2 Suitability of Applying PBL to the International Engineering Management Course  
At the level of course attributes, the match is clear. International engineering management is an 
interdisciplinary subject involving engineering law, engineering economics, project management, 
cross-cultural management, and other fields. Students can hardly understand risk boundaries from 
textbook explanations alone, whereas PBL teach ing based on real cases supports the integrated use of 
multiple knowledge points. 
The model also fits the talent demands of the industry. Various unexpected situations often occur 
during the execution of overseas EPC turnkey projects, and positions require  practitioners to 
independently consult contracts, identify risks, and formulate disposal plans. What PBL trains is 
exactly these core competencies.

Third, PBL makes up for what traditional teaching inherently lacks. Traditional teaching cases are often 
over-idealized, while real projects generally have practical difficulties such as insufficient evidence, 
game-playing among multiple parties, differing understanding of contract clauses, and commercial 
trade-offs. PBL teaching trains students to look at engi neering problems dialectically and keeps them 
from the one-sided cognition that no single formula can settle every claim problem. 
2.3 Risk Allocation Characteristics of the FIDIC 2017 Silver Book EPC Model  
For turnkey general contracting projects, the FIDI C 2017 EPC Silver Book is a common choice, and 
the risk allocation under it is tilted toward the general contractor. The contractor bears most of the 
commercial risks in the design, procurement, and construction stages, but this does not mean that all 
risks are unconditionally transferred to the contractor. Exceptional adverse climatic conditions, force 
majeure, variation instructions issued by the employer, and changes in law still divide the 
responsibilities of each party in accordance with the contract p rovisions. From the authors’ classroom 
teaching experience, many students easily form a cognitive misunderstanding and simply think that 
under the EPC model all risks are borne by the contractor. This is also one of the knowledge points that 
the PBL teaching design of this course aims to correct. 
 
3. Overview of the Teaching Case: The Padma Bridge Highway Link EPC Project in Bangladesh  
3.1 Basic Overview of the Project 
In Bangladesh, the Padma Bridge Highway Link EPC Project counts as a key transportation s cheme 
and stands as a supporting project of China -Bangladesh cooperation under the Belt and Road Initiative. 
The project is located on the alluvial plain of the Padma River, with a total main line length of 55 km. 
The project content includes subgrade eart hwork, bridge and culvert works, pavement works, traffic 
safety facilities, and related ancillary works. This project adopts the EPC turnkey general contracting 
model, and the contract applies the FIDIC 2017 Silver Book. The project signed the contract in June 
2018, started in March 2019, with a planned construction period of 36 months, originally scheduled for 
completion in March 2022. Affected by multiple performance risks, the project was actually opened to 
traffic in December 2022, with a 9 -month delay. The initial contract amount was about US$4.16 billion, 
while the final settlement investment reached about US$4.67 billion, a notable increase in the total 
project investment. 
Bangladesh’s Ministry of Road Transport and Bridges acts as the employer, while  a consortium led by 
European and American consulting agencies provides supervision. A Chinese enterprise acts as the 
EPC general contractor, handling project design, cross -border material procurement, and on -site 
construction delivery, and many local Bang ladeshi subcontractors and labor teams were also brought in. 
The funding comes from Bangladesh’s own fiscal resources and China-Bangladesh bilateral loans. 
The Padma River separates the east and west of Bangladesh, and the ferry crossings used in the past 
were inefficient. Multilateral development institutions once withdrew from the project due to related

3.2 Six Typical Engineering Dispute Events during Project Performance 
During construction, the project met with multiple high -frequency risks typical of overseas EPC 
projects. This paper extracts six groups of PBL inquiry topics fr om them, and each topic corresponds to 
the core teaching knowledge points of the course, with all of them based on publicly available project 
performance materials. 
3.2.1 Topic 1: Time and Cost Claim Dispute Caused by Flood Disaster in an Extremely Long Ra iny 
Season 
In the 2020 and 2021 flood seasons, local rainfall was far above the historical average of the same 
period. The subgrade on the construction site was soaked by floodwater, construction access roads were 
damaged, and subgrade and culvert works we re suspended many times, with cumulative effective 
suspension time exceeding 90 days. The employer and the supervisor regarded the rainy season as a 
routine climate that occurs every year in Bangladesh, refused to approve the extension of the time for 
completion, and did not recognize the related cost compensation. The general contractor submitted 
multiple claim documents in accordance with the contract, but only part of the time extension 
applications were approved, and most of the costs arising from idle labor and equipment were not 
compensated. The knowledge points involved include exceptional adverse climatic conditions, force 
majeure, EPC project claim procedures, and the boundary between time and cost claims.  
3.2.2 Topic 2: Variation Price Dispute Caused by the Employer’s Verbal Instructions 
In the middle and later stages of construction, responding to local livelihood demands, the employer 
issued variations many times through verbal or informal documents, such as local adjustment of road 
alignment, addition of convenience crossings, and widening of non -motorized lanes. The actual on -site 
execution was to carry out the construction first and complete the variation procedures afterwards. 
After the contractor completed the incremental work, some of the  variations did not form formal 
written instructions, and the employer did not confirm the incremental price for these parts, bringing 
evidence difficulties to the settlement work. The knowledge points covered here are the right of 
variation under the EPC model, the validity of verbal instructions, the contract handling of variations 
without written instructions, and the rules of variation valuation. 
3.2.3 Topic 3: Congestion of Cross-border Material Clearance and Supply Chain Interruption Risk 
The main ste el and large pavement equipment were exported from China. The customs inspection 
procedures at Chittagong Port are complex. On top of that, the COVID -19 pandemic caused port 
congestion and rising international shipping prices, and landslides on inland road s during the rainy 
season further hindered material transfer. As a result, equipment and materials stayed at the port for a 
long time, the delivery cycle was extended, and the site had to suspend work waiting for materials. In 
order to catch up with the co nstruction schedule, the contractor could only purchase some substitute

are the allocation of procurement and logistics risks under the EPC model, the risk boundary between

3.2.4 Topic 4: Exchange Loss Risk under the Dual-currency Settlement Model 
The contract settles payments in two currencies, US dollars plus Bangladeshi t aka. Large equipment 
and main materials are settled in US dollars, while local labor and small materials are settled in taka. 
During the project implementation period, the taka continued to depreciate, and the particular 
conditions of the contract did not set up clauses related to exchange rate adjustment. The exchange 
losses caused by the exchange rate decline were mainly borne by the contractor, directly compressing 
the project’s profit margin. The topic covers the international engineering price adjustme nt mechanism, 
exchange rate risk allocation, and the means of exchange rate risk control in the bidding and 
performance stages. 
3.2.5 Topic 5: Conflict of Chinese and Foreign Supervision Standards and Cross -cultural Acceptance 
Disputes 
European and American consulting enterprises make up the bulk of the supervision consortium, and the 
project acceptance follows BS and ASTM standards, which clash with domestic construction processes 
and on-site management habits. After some processes were completed with mat ure domestic techniques, 
the supervisor refused acceptance on the grounds that they did not meet the European and American 
test standards, causing multiple rework and repeated inspection applications. The work habits of the 
Chinese and foreign parties diff er significantly: the foreign party attaches great importance to written 
documents, while the on -site Chinese personnel are accustomed to verbal communication and 
coordination, resulting in low overall communication efficiency. The topic involves the valid ity of 
contractually agreed standards, the boundary of supervision responsibilities, engineering inspection and 
testing, and the paths for handling international engineering disputes. 
3.2.6 Topic 6: Difficulties in Local Labor Management 
Over 90% of the fr ont-line labor was recruited locally in Bangladesh, with uneven skill levels. 
Bangladesh’s labor laws have strict mandatory provisions on rest days, overtime approval, and wage 
protection. In the early stage of the project, the domestic labor management mo del was copied, 
resulting in labor turnover, low work efficiency, and hidden dangers of labor -management conflicts. In 
the later stage, a localized labor management system had to be built, bringing additional management 
costs. The topic corresponds to the contractor’s labor obligations, the contractual responsibility of 
complying with host country laws, and the risk control of local human resources.

4. PBL Teaching Design and Implementation Based on the Padma Project  
4.1 Design of Teaching Objectives 
Following the course syllabus, the teaching objectives are set at three levels: knowledge, ability, and 
quality. 
1) Knowledge objective: after completing the course, students are expected to grasp the key provisions 
of the FIDIC 2017 Silver Book and distin guish how risks are shared between the employer and the 
contractor under the EPC model; master the basic rules of international engineering variation, claim, 
and dispute resolution; and understand common international engineering risks, including exchange 
rates, supply chain, local labor, and cross-cultural management. 
2) Ability objective: students should be able to locate key clauses in contract texts, identify risk points 
from engineering events, formulate disposal plans from the perspective of the EPC c ontractor, and 
show basic abilities in group collaboration and engineering report writing. 
3) Quality objective: students are expected to develop risk awareness for overseas engineering, 
understand the real difficulties of Chinese -funded enterprises going abroad, uphold a spirit of contract, 
foster engineering ethics and patriotic feelings, and carry out the requirements of ideological and 
political education in the course. 
4.2 Pre-class Preparation 
For the teacher, the preparation work includes organizing the public background materials of the 
project, preparing the case task book (which gives only the descriptions of the engineering events, 
without contract analysis or reference answers), preparing the electronic version of the FIDIC Silver 
Book, dividing the six inquiry topics among the learning groups, and preparing the group evaluation 
form and the scoring standards for the output. Since 63.53% of the surveyed students knew nothing 
about PBL before, the task book also includes a one -page introduction to the PBL learning method, 
explaining the basic process of “problem —group—discussion—report,” so that the new classroom 
model will not leave students at a loss. 
For the students, the preparation includes self -studying the basic content of the FIDIC Silver Bo ok 
before class to understand the basic risk framework of EPC general contracting, forming learning 
groups of 5-6 members, and completing the internal role division, with positions such as contract study, 
material collection, report writing, and classroom presentation. Given that 51.76% of the students lack 
prerequisite project management courses and 76.47% have not passed CET -4, the pre -class materials 
add a quick overview of core project management concepts and a Chinese -English comparison table of 
high-frequency FIDIC professional vocabulary, which brings the self -study threshold down to a level 
the students can manage.

4.3 Teaching Implementation Process 
The PBL teaching as a whole runs for 4 class hours in the classroom plus extracurricular group 
discussion. It is divided into five stages: problem release, extracurricular inquiry, classroom output 
report, teacher benchmark comments, and review and summary. 
4.3.1 Stage One: Problem Release (1 class hour in class) 
In this stage, the teacher introduces the overall background of the Padma Bridge Highway Link project, 
issues the case task book, and assigns the six groups of performance disputes to the six learning groups. 
The group tasks are clarified: sort out the cause and development process of the event ; find the 
corresponding contract clauses of the FIDIC Silver Book; identify the risk responsibilities of each party; 
give disposal plans from the perspective of the EPC contractor; and output the group research report 
and prepare the classroom presentatio n PPT. It is also made clear that copying ready -made answers 
from the Internet is forbidden, and every judgment must be supported by contract provisions.  
4.3.2 Stage Two: Extracurricular Group Inquiry (completed independently outside class)  
Offline, each group studies the FIDIC contract, sorts out the event evidence, analyzes the risk 
allocation, and works out solutions through discussion. Teachers conduct online Q&A, giving only 
direction-level guidance without providing standard answers. Combined with th e authors’ actual 
teaching, students are prone to several typical errors: taking it for granted that the contractor bears all 
risks under the EPC model; claiming against the employer directly whenever losses occur; confusing 
exceptional adverse climatic co nditions with force majeure; and ignoring procedural contractual 
constraints such as claim notices and written variation instructions. 
4.3.3 Stage Three: Classroom Output Report (2 class hours in class) 
The groups report one by one, each within 8 -10 minutes. The reporting content includes event sorting, 
contract clause citation, risk identification, and contractor disposal plans. After the report, a classroom 
mutual-questioning session is set up, in which other groups can raise questions and carry out class room 
critical thinking. Taking the flood claim topic as an example, other groups may ask: since Bangladesh 
enters the rainy season every year, shouldn’t the contractor bear all the suspension losses? Such 
questions push the whole class to think deeply. 
4.3.4 Stage Four: Teacher Benchmark Comments (1 class hour in class) 
The teacher then gives benchmark comments, combining the contract basis prepared during lesson 
preparation with the actual handling of the project. The cognitive misunderstandings that commo nly 
appear among students are corrected, the applicable boundary of FIDIC clauses is clarified, the plans 
given by the students are compared with the actual practices on the project site, and the advantages and 
disadvantages of different plans are analyzed . In this stage, the teacher also explains that international 
engineering is not only a game of contract provisions but also has to take commercial reality into 
account, and that many real problems simply have no perfect solution and have to be weighed amo ng 
multiple parties.

4.3.5 Stage Five: Review and After-class Assignment 
The fifth stage assigns review work after class. Each group revises and improves the group report in 
combination with the classroom questions and the teacher’s comments. All students also submit a short 
personal learning reflection summarizing what the risk control of EPC projects has taught them.  
4.4 Assessment and Evaluation Methods 
The course no longer relies on the final exam paper alone, and the PBL teaching outputs are now 
included in the process-based grade. The evaluation includes three parts: the quality of the group report 
and PPT output, the classroom presentation and Q&A performance, and the personal learning 
reflections. The evaluation focus does not require the students’ p lans to be completely consistent with 
the actual practices of the project site, but values more whether the citation of contract clauses is 
accurate, whether the risk identification is complete, and whether the logic of the plans is rigorous. In 
line with the course syllabus, the total grade is composed of the group case presentation (20%), class 
participation (10%), two online tests (20%), and the final examination (50%), and the PBL outputs 
mainly fall into the first two parts. All the task requirements a nd scoring rules are announced to the 
students in the first week of the course. 
 
5. Teaching Practice Effects and Existing Problems 
5.1 Achievements of the Teaching Reform 
Judging from the feedback in the classroom, the reform has brought visible effects in several aspects. 
First, students no longer mechanically memorize FIDIC provisions and can understand the meaning of 
clauses in combination with specific engineering scenarios, correcting typical misconceptions such as 
“under the EPC model the co ntractor bears all risks,” and their contract practice ability has improved. 
Second, through training in the complex scenarios of real overseas projects, students have established 
international engineering risk thinking, recognized the important position o f evidence retention and 
procedural compliance in overseas projects, and understood that international engineering management 
is a comprehensive game of contracts, commerce, and cross -cultural communication. Third, group 
collaboration exercises students’ c omprehensive abilities of division of labor and cooperation, report 
writing, and public presentation; by relying on the real cases of Chinese -funded enterprises under the 
Belt and Road Initiative, the ideological and political education of the course is in tegrated, which gives 
the students a deeper understanding of what China’s overseas engineering contracting industry actually 
does. 
A comparison between the pre -class questionnaire and the classroom feedback shows that the reform 
direction basically matches  the students’ demands. Before class, 71.76% of the students had 
expectations for practical teaching. After a complete round of training, students who had known 
nothing about PBL could independently explain the risk allocation points involved in each topic , and 
the three abilities most demanded in the questionnaire —critical thinking, cross -cultural collaboration,

Specific episodes in the classroom illustrate this point better. During the report on the flood claim topic,

remember knowledge points better than plain clause explanation. 
5.2 Existing Problems in the Teaching Implementation Process 
First, the most prominent problem is the tight class hours. Fully implementing the whole set of PBL 
teaching requires occupying more in -class hours. Under the premise of limited total course hours, the 
time allocation between theoretical teaching and PBL practical teaching needs to be balanced. This 
contradiction had already shown signs in the pre -class questionnaire, where 37.65% of the students 
listed “limited class hours and heavy task pressure” as their main concern about practical teaching.  
Second, the students show a wide gap in contract reading ability. Some students have difficulty reading 
the original foreign -language FIDIC texts, and the depth of clause understanding varies among 
different groups. The questionnaire data and the classroom observati on confirm each other: 76.47% of 
the students have not passed CET -4, and 65.88% of the students admitted having difficulty reading 
English cases and contract clauses. It is unrealistic to expect students to work through the original 
English FIDIC within the class hours available. 
Third, the case materials are limited in an objective sense. Although the case comes from a real project, 
the internal commercial negotiation details and the complete claim reports of the project are non -public 
materials, and students can only rely on public information to complete the deduction. In the pre -class 
questionnaire, 57.65% of the students said they were unfamiliar with international engineering cases. 
Public materials can support the basic deduction, but the complete int ernal commercial logic of the 
project is still out of reach for the students.

6. Optimization Countermeasures for Course Teaching 
The first measure is to strengthen pre -class preview and shorten the in -class time for explaining basic 
concepts. Push the Chinese translation of the FIDIC Silver Book and the project background materials 
before class, require students to complete the preview before class, and leave the classroom time for 
group reports and classroom critical thinking. It is also possible t o select 2 -3 key topics from the six 
topics for complete PBL teaching and assign the remaining topics as after -class extension assignments, 
so as to relieve the pressure of class hours. This arrangement also responds to the preference of 60% of 
the students in the questionnaire for “online self -study plus classroom discussion,” moving the learning 
of basic concepts forward so that the classroom time is used for discussion and reporting.  
As a second measure, a proper learning scaffold should be built for the  students. Provide students with 
the Chinese translation of FIDIC and give clause retrieval guidance to lower the threshold of contract 
study; adopt the principle of matching strong and weak students when grouping, so as to drive the 
students with weak foundations to participate in group inquiry. The questionnaire shows that 65.88% of 
the students have difficulty reading English contracts and 85.88% worry about insufficient professional 
vocabulary. The Chinese translation and the clause retrieval guidance target these weak points directly. 
Teaching resources also need to be enriched, which is the third measure, so that virtual and real cases 
complement each other. In addition to the Padma project, introduce simplified cases of Belt and Road 
projects such as the Jakarta -Bandung High -Speed Railway and the China -Laos Railway to build the 
course case library; push the literature on Chinese -funded enterprises’ overseas EPC performance after 
class as extended reading materials. In the questionnaire, 61.18% of the s tudents hoped to hear online 
sharing from industry experts, and 57.65% of the students felt unfamiliar with international engineering 
cases. A case library combining virtual and real cases, together with experts entering the classroom, can 
answer both demands. 
The ideological and political education of the course should continue as well. In the case discussion, 
students are guided to think about the opportunities and challenges faced by Chinese -funded enterprises 
going abroad, with the aim of strengthening their contract awareness, risk awareness, and patriotic 
feelings. 
Bilingual teaching should also advance step by step instead of blindly raising the proportion of English. 
The questionnaire shows that 85.88% of the students worry about insufficient profess ional vocabulary, 
and nearly 70% of the students report difficulty in reading and writing English contracts. Bilingual

7. Conclusion 
Training professional talents for the foreign engineering field  is the task of the International 
Engineering Management course, and the traditional theoretical teaching model alone cannot fully 
exercise the students’ engineering practical ability. The PBL problem -oriented teaching model uses the 
performance conflicts of real overseas EPC projects as the driver, guides students to study FIDIC 
contracts independently, identify risks, and deduce disposal plans, and turns theoretical knowledge into 
practical ability. 
With the Padma Bridge Highway Link EPC Project in Bangla desh as the case, this study identifies six 
types of real engineering problems —claim disputes, variation price disputes, supply chain risks, 
exchange losses, cross -cultural acceptance conflicts, and local labor management —and completes the 
whole set of PBL  teaching design. Classroom practice shows that this teaching model can deepen 
students’ understanding of the risk allocation rules of the EPC model under the FIDIC Silver Book, 
break the idealized cognition of textbooks, and improve the comprehensive prof essional ability of 
students. This teaching model also faces practical problems such as tight class hours and uneven 
contract reading ability among students, which can be continuously optimized through pre -class 
preview, selection of teaching topics, and b uilding learning scaffolds. The pre -course questionnaire 
provided a clear learning -situation coordinate for this round of reform. The students’ expectations and 
concerns about practical teaching were finally turned into specific arrangements in the teachin g design, 
which also makes the reform measures clearly targeted. 
In the coming teaching rounds, the authors will continue to explore real engineering projects under the 
Belt and Road Initiative, keep enriching the PBL teaching case resources, and advance t he teaching 
reform of the International Engineering Management course, with the aim of cultivating more
