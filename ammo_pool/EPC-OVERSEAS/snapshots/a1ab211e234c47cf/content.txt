MARKET OVERVIEW 
The energy sector in Thailand appears to be at a crossroads since the inception of our Energy Transition 
Report 2023 and our most recent iteration in early 2024. The sector is now faced with a number of challenges 
including (as of the date of this report) increasing pressure from the Thai government to reduce electricity 
prices from THB4.15 per unit to THB3.70 in order to address cost-of-living concerns, stable but unremarkable 
GDP growth, and ample power supply, as evidenced by a power reserve margin in excess of 25%. The 
proposed reduction in electricity prices has had a dampening effect on the electricity generation sector, as 
the share prices for many publicly liste d power developers have experienced significant declines as part of 
ongoing concerns about any potential reduced revenue. Numerous questions in the sector abound.  What 
is the Thai government’s approach to the VSPP and SPP power purchase agreements (“PPAs”) which have 
formed the cornerstone of the electricity generation sector in Thailand?  Will the PPAs be renegoti ated or 
terminated?  What recourse is available to the private sector, and how will developers respond?  How will 
the lenders to these projects be affected? The risks to market participants, new and old, are considerable.  
Notwithstanding this, the opportunities in this sector remain as Thailand continues its path towards further 
decarbonisation. Last year, the Energy Regulatory Commission (“ERC”) announced the winners of its second 
tender for 2.2 GW of renewable energy cons isting of 600MW wind and 1,580 MW of solar capacity under a 
feed-in tariff scheme.  We expect a third -round tender, consisting of 1,488 MW of renewable energy, to be 
announced later this year. 
The continuing proliferation of EV sales in Thailand has been a prevailing theme for the past three years and 
will continue to drive energy demand in 2025.

NATURAL RESOURCES 
Overview 
Thailand's oil and gas sector is at a critical juncture, facing the sunset of existing concessions and a wave of 
decommissioning activities, while still possessing untapped natural resources hindered by diplomatic 
challenges. Rising LNG import costs have p rompted companies like PTT Group to boost production and 
initiate significant changes.  Concurrently, international pressure and the urgent need to meet net zero 
targets have pressured the government to expedite regulatory reforms for industry decarbonisati on. In 
September 2024, Prime Minister Paetongtarn Shinawatra’s government announced policies to reduce 
energy prices, restructure energy pricing, develop strategic petroleum reserves, explore additional 
resources, and address the Thailand-Cambodia overlapping claims area. Emerging technologies are pivotal; 
however, regulatory developments are delayed, with comprehensive legislation anticipated by late 2025.  
Amidst these challenges, new industry players, predominantly those with state -aligned interests, hold  the 
potential to drive transformative change, setting the stage for a dynamic energy landscape in Thailand.  
Upstream Oil & Gas Government 
Policies
As Thailand navigates the complexities of the global 
energy landscape, the government has laid out 
comprehensive policies and plans to ensure energy 
security and sustainability. The NEPC’s over-arching 
policies, known as Thailand’s Integrated Energy 
Blueprint (TIEB) with five separate policies (namely, 
Thailand’s Power Development Plan (PDP), the 
Energy Efficiency Plan, the Alternative Energy 
Development Plan, the Oil Plan and the Gas Plan), 
are periodically updated. A new PDP 2024 and Gas 
Plan 2024 are cur rently in the pipeline. The recent 
draft PDP 2024, covering the period between 2024-
2037, aims to increase the proportion of renewable 
energy to 51% by 2037, up from 20% in the 
previous plan. Gas will make up 41%, reflecting a 
slight decrease from 57% in 2023. Coal is projected 
to decrease to 7% by 2037 down from 20% in the 
previous plan, while nuclear energy remains at 1%. 
This plan is part of a broader strategy to achieve 
carbon neutrality by 2050.

Investment Incentives 
In 2023, petroleum concessionaires, a PSC 
contractor, and direct contractors were granted 
rights to bring foreign experts and skilled workers 
into the country. In accordance with Sections 69 and 
70 of the Petroleum Act 1971, exemptions from 
import duties and VAT were granted for specialists 
and skilled technicians, as well as for supplies and 
machinery. No other incentives currently exist for 
upstream oil and gas.

Petroleum Bidding
Tasked with the action plan to ‘establish and 
maintain security of mineral fuel supply from 
indigenous and overseas sources’ the DMF has 
launched multiple recent and ongoing initiatives 
aimed at accelerating investment and raising 
production rates. 
24th Bidding Round 
In 2022, the DMF launched the 24 th bidding round 
for petroleum exploration and production in the 
Gulf of Thailand in the form of a production sharing 
contract, covering: Block G1/65, the previously 
relinquished South Jamjuree field; Block G2/65, the 
southern part of the Pattani Basin to the west of 
Block B12/27; and Block G3/65, which 
encompasses parts of the Northern Malay Basin 
adjacent to Bongkot (Blocks 15, 16, and 17), 
surrounding Block G11/48. (the “24th Bidding”). The 
24th Bidding concluded in early 2023, with three 
PSCs awarded and executed by PTTEP Energy

and Chevron Offshore (Thailand) Limited (Block 
G2/65).  
According to the operating plans submitted by the

Decommissioning in Thailand and offshore oil and 
gas operations remains a complex, resource -
intensive process, requiring flexibility and creativity 
due to regional challenges, technical and 
regulatory complexities, and the involvement of the 
DMF. In 2023, extensions were granted due to 
delays, while successful repurposing projects, such 
as rig -to-reef and topsides reuse, helped avoid 
2,500 tonnes of steel waste, highlighting the 
importance of innovative approaches in the energy 
transition. 
It is observed that local capabilities for 
decommissioning work remain limited in Thailand, 
and that engineering consultants and service 
providers for projects in Thailand typically come 
from other countries such as the United States, the 
United Kingdom, Malaysia, and China. 
The issue of residual liability following 
decommissioning continues to pose a challenge, 
particularly when coupled with the need to 
introduce CCS/CCUS laws. It is unclear what the 
implications are if CCS operators re -open 
abandoned wells or utilise old fac ilities left by 
previous operators, or whether CCS obligations will 
be linked to exploration and production rights 
going forward.

New laws impacting the industry 
A number of new laws impacting the industry over 
the past few years have now been tested in practice. 
Decommissioning regulations introduced in 2017 
are being applied to expiring concessions. The 
requirement for upfront decommissioning security 
remains a point of contention among 
concessionaires and the government. While studies 
and proposed amendments hav e been proposed, 
no efforts to change this requirement seem 
forthcoming in the near future. However, since new 
bidding rounds have been made under the PSC 
scheme, and the PSC itself contains provisions for 
the PSC contractor to put in place decommissioning 
funds, the risk of similar conflicts with the 
government over placing of decommissioning 
security may be lower. 
The new set of competition regulations issued in 
2022, which empowered the ERC to regulate 
merger controls of ERC -licensed businesses in the 
electricity and natural gas industries, are being 
applied. On average, ERC merger approvals take 
up to 6 months. In  addition to regulations on ERC 
merger controls, foreign business restrictions were 
anticipated, although progress on these remains 
unclear and no estimated timeline has been 
provided. 
Towards the end of 2023, regulations related to 
licensing for pool gas managers were introduced. 
In August 2024, the ERC conducted a public 
hearing on the draft Notification on the Standard of 
Gas Sale Agreements between Natural Gas 
Procurement and Wholesale Licensees and Users of 
Natural Gas. The government is under significant 
pressure to make energy and fuel prices 
competitive and manageable. To attract 
competition from the private sector, the 
government also seeks to revamp the way gas is 
distributed ac ross various sectors to reduce 
electricity expenses. Pool gas pricing and 
arrangements average out the cost of gas from 
those procured from the Gulf of Thailand and those 
that are imported from Myanmar or as LNG. 
However, the existing Transmission System 
Operator Code was not designed to accommodate 
a market where PTT is not the only transmission 
system operator. Thus, it remains to be seen how 
the liberalisation of the gas sales and distribution 
market will function. 
In conjunction with the matters above, amendments 
to the Petroleum Act, the Energy Industry Act, and 
the implementation of the Clean Air Act, among 
other related pieces of legislation, are currently 
being studied. These pieces of legislation aim to 
establish legal frameworks for new energies like 
CCS/CCUS, Hydrogen, and carbon taxation and 
trading. Academic proposals have been submitted 
to the ERC for further action in the second half of 
2024. If ordinary legislative process and timelines 
are followed, we d o not expect these laws to be 
issued within the first half of 2025.  
 
Final thoughts 
As Thailand's existing oil and gas concessions 
approach their sunset phase, the country faces a 
dual challenge: managing a wave of 
decommissioning activities while tapping into its 
remaining natural resources, some of which are 
hindered by diplomatic complexities.  
The increased reliance on expensive LNG imports 
has prompted national petroleum companies, such 
as PTT Group, to intensify their oil and gas 
production efforts and initiate various change

CLIMATE CHANGE AND CARBON REDUCTION 
Thailand has been taking significant steps to balance economic growth with environmental protection. The 
introduction of several new environmental laws underscores the country’s commitment to a sustainable 
future. 
This section will examine key developments in Thailand's environmental laws, such as the draft Climate 
Change Act, the framework for voluntary carbon credits, the draft Clean Air Act, and the Thailand Taxonomy. 
These initiatives reflect Thailand's proactive app roach to integrating environmental sustainability into its 
legal and economic frameworks. 
 
Draft Climate Change Act  
The Draft Climate Change Act is a comprehensive 
legislative framework designed to address the 
escalating crisis of climate change. The Act aims to 
reduce greenhouse gas emissions, promote 
sustainable development, and enhance the 
country's resilience to cli mate impacts. It 
emphasises the transition to a low-carbon economy 
while ensuring climate justice. 
Key Objectives 
The primary objectives of the Act are to prevent and 
mitigate the adverse effects of climate change, align 
with national commitments, and ensure the 
sustainability of natural resources and the 
environment. The Act sets short, medium, and long-
term goals for reducing greenhouse gas emissions, 
achieving carbon neutrality by 2050, and 
accomplishing net -zero emissions by 2065. It also 
mandates the integration of these goals into the 
plans and budgets of all relevant government 
agencies. 
To achieve these objectives, the Act introduces 
several mechanisms, including the establishment of 
a National Committee on Climate Change, chaired 
by the Prime Minister, to oversee and coordinate 
climate policies. It also proposes the creation of a 
Climate Fund to finance initiatives aimed at 
reducing emissions and enhancing climate 
resilience. The fund will be sourced from carbon 
taxes, emission trading schemes, government 
subsidies, and private donations. 
Greenhouse Gas Emission Database 
The draft Climate Change Act mandates the 
creation of a comprehensive greenhouse gas 
(GHG) emission database for Thailand. This 
database will include detailed information on GHG 
emissions, sequestration, and reduction activities 
across various sectors, inc luding energy, 
transportation, industry, agriculture, forestry, land 
use, and waste management. The Department of 
Climate Change and Environment will have the 
authority to collect data from relevant state 
agencies and private entities, ensuring the 
database adheres to international standards and is 
publicly accessible. The database will serve as a 
foundational tool for developing and implementing 
GHG reduction measures and policies. 
Emission Trading Scheme System 
The Emission Trading Scheme (ETS) is a key 
component of the draft Climate Change Act, 
designed to control and reduce GHG emissions 
through a market-based approach. The ETS will set 
a cap on the total amount of GHG emissions 
allowed and allocate emission al lowances across 
various sectors. These allowances can be traded 
among entities, creating economic incentives for 
reducing emissions. The system also includes

mechanisms for monitoring, reporting, and 
verifying emissions, as well as penalties for non -
compliance. The ETS aims to ensure that Thailand 
meets its national and international GHG reduction 
targets efficiently and cost-effectively. 
Cross Border Adjustment Mechanism 
The Cross Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) 
is introduced to prevent carbon leakage, which 
occurs when production shifts to countries with less 
stringent climate policies, leading to increased 
global emissions. Under the CBAM, imported 
goods will be subject to a carbon price adjustment 
based on their GHG emissions. Importers will be 
required to register, report the emissions 
associated with their products, and pay a fee 
equivalent to the carbon price in Thailand. This 
mechanism ensures a level playing fiel d for 
domestic industries and encourages global 
emission reductions. 
Carbon Tax 
The draft Act proposes a carbon tax on goods that 
emit GHGs during their lifecycle. This tax will be 
levied on manufacturers and importers based on 
the quantity of goods produced or imported, with 
rates specified in ministerial regulations. The 
carbon tax aims to internalise the environmental 
cost of GHG emissions, incentivising businesses to 
adopt cleaner technologies and reduce their 
carbon footprint. Revenue from the carbon tax will 
be directed to the treasury, providing a stable 
source of funding for climate initiatives. 
Carbon Credit 
The draft Act establishes a framework for managing 
and utilising carbon credits, which can be 
generated from projects that reduce emissions and 
can be traded or used to offset emissions in the ETS. 
The framework includes standards for verification, 
monitoring, and reporting to ensure transparency. 
The goal is to support domestic emission reduction 
projects and position Thailand in the global carbon 
market. 
Taxonomy 
The draft Act mandates the development of a 
taxonomy to classify economic activities based on 
their environmental and climate impacts. This 
taxonomy will provide clear definitions, criteria, and 
indicators for assessing the sustainability of various 
economic activities. It aims to guide policy -making, 
investment decisions, and the allocation of financial 
resources towards activities that support climate 
goals. The taxonomy will be developed with input 
from stakeholders and will align with international 
standards. 
Current Status 
The Department of Climate Change and

hearing. The National Committee on Climate

and has tasked the Ministry of Natural Resources

Carbon credit framework  
As global attention shifts to clean energy and a low-
carbon economy, businesses are considering how 
carbon markets may help them achieve their 
decarbonisation initiatives and emission reduction 
goals. In this respect, Thailand's voluntary carbon 
market off ers a platform for companies to acquire 
carbon credits from independent projects 
dedicated to reducing or eliminating greenhouse 
gas emissions. 
There are two primary types of carbon markets: 
regulatory compliance markets and voluntary 
markets. Regulatory compliance markets are legally 
enforced, with consequences for failing to meet 
emission reduction targets and benefits for 
successful compliance.  In contrast, voluntary 
carbon markets, like Thailand's, operate without 
any legal requirement on companies to reduce 
emissions, but rather, offer an independent 
platform for GHG emissions reduction projects. In 
Thailand, carbon credits are tradable certif icates 
representing the reduction, or removal of 
greenhouse gas emissions, typically on a tCO2eq 
basis.

various sectors. Certified carbon credits generated 
through T-VER can be used for offsetting emissions 
or trading within Thailand. T -VER project 
development consists of two main steps: (i) project 
registration; and (ii) greenhouse gas certification. 
Projects, including those in forestry, agriculture, 
waste management, renewable energy, and more, 
can be registered with TGO by the project

Premium T-VER 
The Premium Thailand Voluntary Emission

aligning with international standards and 
enhancing the quality of carbon credits. Premium T-

and other international frameworks, ensuring that 
the carbon credits generated are of high quality and 
widely accepted.

guide the sel ection of greenhouse gas (GHG)

methodologies for calculating, monitoring, and 
verifying GHG emission reductions. 
Projects eligible for Premium T-VER must fall under 
specific categories, including renewable energy, 
energy efficiency, public transportation, electric 
vehicles, waste management, and forestry and 
agriculture, among others. The program covers 
seven types o f GHGs, including carbon dioxide 
(CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O). 
Projects can be developed as single projects, 
bundling projects, or program of activities (PoA),

additionality and permanence of GHG reductions.

program emphasises the importance of avoiding 
negative impacts on the environment and local 
communities, ens uring that projects do no net

enhance the social and environmental co -benefits 
of the projects, making them more attractive to 
buyers and stakeholders.

Carbon Capture and Storage and 
Carbon Capture, Utilization, and 
Storage
Overview 
Thailand has committed to achieving carbon 
neutrality by 2050. As the country seeks to shift from 
fossil fuels to renewable energy sources, CCUS 
technologies, piloted by PTT Group, the country’s 
national energy company, provide a potential 
pathway to manag e and mitigate emissions from 
existing industrial processes and energy 
production. These solutions are crucial for reducing 
Thailand’s significant CO2 emissions while 
addressing the limitations of its natural carbon 
absorption capacity, enabling Thailand t o meet its 
carbon neutrality goals without compromising 
economic growth or energy security. 
Understanding CCUS Technologies 
CCUS technologies encompass two primary 
approaches: CCS and CCUS. Both methods begin 
with capturing carbon dioxide (CO2) from industrial 
sources or directly from the atmosphere, but their 
post-capture processes differ: 
Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS):  This 
approach involves the permanent storage of CO2 in 
secure geological formations, such as depleted oil 
and gas fields, deep salient aquifers, and 
unmineable coal seams, preventing it from 
returning to the atmosphere.  
 
Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage (CCUS): 
In this approach, captured CO2 is either repurposed 
into valuable products such as chemicals, fuels, and 
construction materials, used for enhanced oil 
recovery, or stored. However, transforming CO2 
requires substantial energy, which can increase 
production costs. 
The overall process of CCUS involves four key 
steps:  
1. Capture – this includes both pre - and post - 
combustion capture of CO2; 
2. Compression and Transport – where captured 
CO2 is compressed into a supercritical state to 
reduce its volume, making it easier to transport. 
It is then transported via pipelines, ships, or 
other means to a storage or utilisation site. 
3. Utilization – where captured CO2 is used; and 
4. Storage – where captured CO2 is injected into a

Overview

Ammonia (NH3) and hydrogen are closely linked, as 
ammonia’s components include hydrogen and 
nitrogen. Hydrogen is a lightweight and carbon -

the transportation and storage of hydrogen due to

Hydrogen can be stored in ammonia through the 
ammonia synthesis process, which combines 
hydrogen gas with nitrogen gas to produce 
ammonia. Then, hydrogen can be extracted when 
and where it is needed by heating ammonia to high 
temperatures. Therefore, the storage of hydrogen 
in the form of ammonia could mitigate the practical 
challenges associated with transporting and storing 
hydrogen.

Overview 
Battery energy storage systems (the “BESS”) is

sustainable energy future by enabling the storage 
of electricity generated from variable renewable 
energy sources. While BESS offers a significant 
advantage by enhancing energy security when 
used alongside renewable power projects, the 
production, use, and disposal of batteries may have 
environmental impacts, e.g., batteries may contain 
materials that can contaminate soil and water. 
 
Current Incentives for BESS Manufacturing 
The Board of Investment (the "BOI") offers generous 
incentives for the manufacturing of energy storage 
systems, although these incentives do not extend to 
businesses that install BESS. BOI incentives which 
are in effect for BESS manufacturing include: 
• high-density battery manufacturing that 
includes cell processes is eligible for A1 
privileges, which provide an exemption from 
corporate income tax for eight years without a 
cap. Additionally, manufacturers benefit from a 
90% reduction in import duties for raw materials 
and essential materials not produced 
domestically, approved annually for five years. 
• high-density battery storage manufacturing 
using cells as materials (e.g. modules or battery 
packs) is eligible for A2 privileges. These 
include an eight -year corporate income tax 
exemption with a cap equivalent to net profits, 
along with the same 90% redu ction in import 
duties for raw materials and essential materials 
for five years. 
• high-density battery storage manufacturing 
using modules to produce battery packs will be 
eligible for A3 privileges, which include the 
exemption of corporate income tax for five 
years.

DATA CENTRES AND THAILAND’S ENERGY TRANSITION 
Data centres are a cornerstone of Thailand's digital economy, facilitating services such as cloud computing, 
artificial intelligence, and advanced manufacturing. However, the energy -intensive nature of these facilities 
poses a significant challenge to the country’s energy transition goals, which prioritise integrating renewable 
energy and reducing carbon emissions. With Thailand’s Power Development Plan (PDP) emphasising 
sustainability, the intersection of data centre growth and energy transition initiative s demands careful 
consideration. 
Globally, data centres account for approximately 1 -2% of electricity consumption, according to the 
International Energy Agency (IEA). In Thailand, this figure is compounded by the tropical climate, which 
necessitates energy-intensive cooling systems. The r ise of technologies like blockchain and AI has further 
exacerbated electricity demand, making it imperative for operators and policymakers to address these 
challenges while balancing economic and environmental priorities.  
Between January and November 2024, the Board of Investment (BOI) approved 47 data centre and cloud 
service projects valued at THB 173 billion. Major investors include companies from the United States, 
Australia, China, Singapore, Japan, and India, signalin g robust international interest in Thailand as a data 
centre hub.

Challenges in Integrating Energy 
Transition Goals 
While Thailand has made significant strides in 
supporting data centres and energy transition, 
challenges remain: 
High Cooling Energy Demand: Thailand's tropical 
climate necessitates energy -intensive cooling 
systems. Innovations such as AI -powered cooling 
and liquid-cooling systems are being adopted, but

cost barriers.

power supply while reducing dependency on fossil 
fuels is a complex balancing act. 
Regulatory Framework:  Although Thailand has 
introduced initiatives like UGT and Direct PPA,

bolster Thailand's position as a sustainable data

The growth of data centres in Thailand presents a

practices within the sector. However, addressing 
challenges related to cooling energy demand,

ELECTRICITY GENERATION 
Overview 
Under Thailand’s Power Development Plan 2018 (Revision 1), the country has set a target to reduce CO 2 
emissions from the power sector to 47.4 million tonnes of CO 2 equivalent by 2050. Power generation, 
currently the largest source of CO 2 emissions in Thailand, accounted for 89.6 million tonnes in 2023. 23 The 
transportation sector follows as the second -highest contributor to emissions. To address this, the Thai 
government has announced ambitious plans to roll out the adoption of electric vehicles (see below). As such, 
decarbonising the grid will be essenti al for Thailand to achieve its National Determined Contributions 
(NDCs). In this section, we will examine key aspects of the power sector in Thailand at present, as well as 
policy initiatives being developed that will enable Thailand to accelerate its deca rbonisation objectives. 
 
Natural Gas Outlook 
As mentioned above in this report, natural gas 
remains the key energy source driving Thailand’s 
economy. This is unlikely to change within the next 
10-15 years, as the new infrastructure needed to 
support the transition to low carbon fuels will take 
some time to ramp up. In 2023, natural gas 
continued to dominate Thailand’s energy mix, 
accounting for 42% of the country’s total p rimary 
commercial energy consumption and serving as the 
largest source of electricity generation. 24 
With domestic reserves being depleted, the Thai 
energy sector is increasingly reliant on imported 
natural gas; unfortunately, Thailand’s newfound 
reliance comes at an inopportune time. Domestic 
upstream production, particularly in the Erawan gas 
field, has dropped significantly over the past several 
years. This drop has been exacerbated by the 
expiration of the concession contract previously 
held by Chevron Thailand Exploration & 
Production, Ltd. for the Erawan area in the Gulf of 
Thailand in 2022.With prov en and probable 
reserves being depleted, this trend is likely to 
continue into the foreseeable future.

Utility Green Tariff 
A utility green tariff is a voluntary program provided 
by local utilities in various countries, whereby 
renewable energy certificates (“RECs”) are issued to 
power purchasers to signify that a specific portion 
of the power they consume comes from renewable 
sources. This program enables businesses, as 
customers, to claim to be buying renewable energy 
exclusively or at a specified level in order to power 
their operations, and therefore presumably lower 
their Scope 2 carbon emissions. Depending on the 
requirements of the utility green tariff scheme, 
typically renewable energy can be generated either 
by projects owned by the local utility or by 
independent producers in a regulated electricity 
market. Generally, there are three main models of 
utility green tariff programs, i.e., (i) a sleeved power

utility may manage the power supply on the 
customer’s behalf by way of entering into power 
PPAs with independent power producers; (ii) a 
subscriber model where the customers subscribe to 
a portion of larger re newable energy projects with 
the local utility; and (iii) a market-based rate model, 
where the customers are allowed to contract with 
project developers directly or purchase electricity 
at wholesale prices. 27 Each scheme will determine 
the characteristics of the utility green tariff 
programs being offered such as the capacity of the 
program, contract period, type of resources and

Every green tariff program will establish its own 
unique set of regulations, procedures and eligibility 
criteria. In this regard, if participation in the 
program is limited to certain customers (e.g.,

the standards for determining which customers 
qualify to purchase utility green tariffs within the 
respective program. 
A utility green tariff scheme is intended as an 
alternative for buyers to procure renewable power 
for their operations without having to be co-located 
with a renewable power plant (e.g. rooftop solar) or 
supply power directly from a renewable generator. 
It also allows companies to gain access to 
renewable power more easily, especially for those 
who are aiming to lower their carbon footprint to 
meet their sustainability or decarbonisation goals

policies.

There are few locations in Thailand where wind 
speeds would render wind power economical. The 
northeast of the country is the site of most of the 
current onshore wind projects. Although there are 
currently no offshore wind projects under 
development, the n ation's extensive coastlines 
present a potential opportunity for this sustainable 
energy solution.

of Natural Resources and Environmental Policy and 
Planning, Thailand has wind potential with an

development rights in specific offshore blocks

projects. In addition, offshore power projects would

TRANSPORTATION 
Thailand is at the forefront of an ambitious energy transition, with a strong emphasis on transforming its 
transportation sector. The nation’s commitment to combat climate change and reduce GHG emissions is 
underscored by strategic initiatives aimed at rev olutionising its transport ecosystem. According to the 
Thailand’s Mid -century, Long -term Low Greenhouse Gas Emission Development Strategy dated October  
2022 submitted under the Paris Agreement, Thailand’s national greenhouse gas inventories can be 
categorised into five sectors of sources and sinks: (i) energy; (ii) industrial processes and product use (IPPU); 
(iii) agriculture; (iv) land use, land -use change and forestry (LULUCF); and (v) waste. Notably, the energy 
sector holds the largest share, with the t ransport sector being a significant contributor to increased energy 
consumption and GHG emissions.31 
According to the Asian Development Bank, among transport modes, road transport accounts for 26% of the 
country’s annual GHG emissions. 32 Tackling emissions in this sector is therefore vital to Thailand’s efforts to 
combat climate change and reach its GHG emissions reduction goals by  2030.

Thailand's Leap into EVs 
Thailand has solidified its standing as the 
preeminent car manufacturer in the region, 
boasting an impressive output of over 1.6 million 
vehicles in 2021. Recognised as an automotive hub 
of Asia, Thailand is poised to spearhead the global 
shift towards electrification.  
However, there exist significant challenges ahead, 
notably the relatively elevated cost of electric 
vehicles (“EV”) and the imperative for an expanded 
public charging infrastructure. To bolster the long -
term prosperity of the EV industry, the government 
has implemented measures such as subsidies for EV 
purchases and providing customs duty exemptions 
for imported parts utilised in EV production. As 
highlighted in our report last year, ‘Thailand's 
Energy Transition – 2024 Outlook,’ Thailand’s 
National EV Poli cy Committee has announced an 
ambitious roadmap of Thailand’s EV development 
spanning from 2021 to 2035, with the objective of 
developing a well -established supply chain for 
manufacturing EVs. On the demand side, in 
February of 2022, the government announc ed an 
incentive package, including a reduction in import 
duty for completely built-up battery electric vehicles 
(BEVs), an excise tax cut for imported EVs, and an 
EV subsidy for passenger cars. This subsidy 
program helped put 77,741 BEVs on the road in the 
first ten months of 2023 which are far above the 
20,816 units in the whole 2022.  
In 2024, the Thai government reaffirmed its 
commitment to green transportation by allocating 
THB 7.12 billion for an EV subsidy program. This 
initiative provides financial support for the purchase 
of both EVs and electric motorcycles, with over THB 
6.87 billion already disbursed to date. The program 
is a key element in advancing Thailand’s ambitious 
goal to ensure that 30% of domestic vehicle 
production consists of electric vehicles by 2030. It 
also aims to stimulate the domestic EV production 
sector and si gnificantly contribute to the reduction 
of greenhouse gas emissions, aligning with the 
country’s broader sustainability and climate change 
mitigation objectives.

Other legal and policy updates 
New notifications issued in 2023 and 2024 
To improve the supply chain and support the 
increasing demand for EVs by making the transition 
more affordable for both consumers and 
manufacturers, the Ministry of Finance issued a 
notification in 2023 regarding the exemption of 
import duties on parts for  BEVs and battery -
powered boats. This exemption will be effective 
from 26 May 2023 until 31 December 2025.  
To qualify for import duty exemptions on parts for 
BEVs and battery -powered boats, the imported 
parts must satisfy two key conditions: 
• certification by the Thailand Automotive 
Institute; and 
• assembly or utilisation in the production of 
BEVs or battery-powered boats within one year 
of importation. 
If these conditions are not fulfilled within the one -
year deadline, importers are required to either 
export the parts or pay the full amount of duties 
previously exempted. The parts eligible for duty 
exemptions include batteries, traction motors, EV 
compressors, battery management systems, driving 
control systems, on -board chargers, DC/DC 
converters, inverters, power conditioning units 
(PCUs), and reduction gears. 
On April 11, 2024, the National Competitiveness 
Enhancement for Targeted Industries Committee 
issued Notification No. 1/2024 to promote 
investment in the EV supply chain, specifically 
targeting manufacturers of advanced cells for EVs 
and Energy Storage Sys tems (ESS). To qualify for 
this promotion, battery manufacturers must meet 
the following criteria: 
• products must be of high quality and 
recognised by EV manufacturers; 
• production must include cell -level batteries for 
EVs or ESS;  
• batteries must have a specific energy density of 
at least 150 Wh/kg; and 
• batteries must endure over 1,000 recharge 
cycles, as stipulated in the Notification. 
Eligible manufacturers may access several 
incentives, including funding for investment and 
R&D, exemptions from corporate income tax and 
dividend tax, import duty exemptions, and the right 
to own land in Thailand. These measures aim to 
strengthen Thailand's position as a key player in the 
EV and ESS supply chains and to encourage 
sustainable growth in advanced energy 
technologies.

Final thoughts 
Thailand’s integration of electric modes of transport 
into public transport networks is a key step toward 
achieving broader sustainability goals and fostering 
a cleaner environment. Beyond the immediate 
reduction in tailpipe emissions, this initiative 
influences broader energy systems and supports 
the growth of clean energy infrastructure. In this 
symbiotic relationship, electric public 
transportation has emerged as a driving force 
behind energy transition efforts. The collective 
impact of widespread adoption of green mobility is 
crucial for moving t owards a low -carbon future, 
contributing to cleaner air, reducing dependence 
on finite fossil fuels, and mitigating the global 
impact of climate change. 
Thailand’s commitment to becoming the EV hub of 
Southeast Asia is evident through its 
comprehensive policy framework, strategic 
incentives, and collaborative initiatives. While 
challenges like limited infrastructure and concerns 
about EV usage persist, Tha iland’s vision for a 
sustainable, green future in transportation is well 
within reach. The transition to electric vehicles 
marks a significant milestone in the automotive 
industry, setting a precedent for sustainable, 
forward-thinking transportation solutions. 
However, challenges such as limited infrastructure 
and skepticism about EV usage persist. Thailand's 
EV market growth is still developing, influenced by 
the dominance of internal combustion engine (ICE)-
powered cars, which remain competitively priced. 
While high-income individuals are early adopters of 
EVs, the charging infrastructure gap remains a 
significant hurdle for broader adoption. 
To address these challenges, Thailand needs 
streamlined policies, enhanced product 
manufacturing, and improved infrastructure for 
mass EV adoption. Incentive measures, including 
subsidies and tax incentives, should be maintained 
to make EVs more affordable  for consumers and 
attract investments in local EV production. 
Infrastructure developments, such as charging 
stations and upgraded grids, must be promoted to 
respond to the increasing use of EVs on the road. 
More importantly, consumer education will become 
a top priority for automakers, as providing clear 
information about EV advantages can bridge the 
knowledge gap, dispel myths, and empower 
consumers to make informed decisions about 
adopting EVs.
