Abstract
This study focuses on the application of FIDIC Conditions of Contract for EPC Turnkey Projects—Silver Book 1999, 
(FIDIC (EPC&T)), when governed by the Egyptian Law of Contracts concluded by public bodies No. 182 of 2018 (ELC) 
due to the conflicts that may arise from such application. Hence, this study identifies specific FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 
clauses that have the potential for conflicts with ELC, shows the similarities and disparities between these clauses and 
the relevant ELC articles, and provides recommendations to ensure the successful application of FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 
under ELC. This was achieved through a multistep research methodology in which a survey was undertaken to explore 
the perspectives of a selected group of construction contracts’ experts about the conflicting clauses of FIDIC (EPC&T) 
1999 with ELC’s articles. Next, based on the survey results, the clauses related to delay damages, adjustment for changes 
in cost, and contract termination were analyzed under the ELC articles and, consequently, some amendments are recom -
mended for some of these FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses to make them more suitable for use under ELC. Accordingly, 
through applying these amendments, this study will contribute to the wider application of FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 in Egypt 
as it helps in reducing the possibilities of legal conflicts and enhances the contractual certainty in public EPC projects. 
In addition, the findings of this study contribute to the body of knowledge related to construction contracts by offering a 
framework for assessing the application of international forms of contracts under national procurement laws.

(3) The contract includes exceptional terms: These are

Thus, according to the preceding, any contract containing 
all those three characteristics is deemed a public contract; 
conversely, in the absence of any of these three character -
istics, the contract may be construed as a private contract.
FIDIC application in Egypt’ s legal system:
The FIDIC contract forms are based upon the common 
law principles originally established by the Institution of 
Civil Engineers (ICE) [3, 6, 7] and are the most widely used 
form of contract for international construction projects [ 8, 
9]. Each FIDIC contract form is suitable for a specific proj-
ect delivery method; for instance, the FIDIC EPC&T (Sil -
ver Book) is designed for turnkey projects and is commonly 
used in publicly funded infrastructure projects [ 10, 11]. In 
Egypt, due to the absence of a uniform public works con -
tract, many major projects follow FIDIC forms, including 
the Grand Egyptian Museum, Hurghada Airport expansion, 
Cairo Metro, and projects funded by international develop -
ment agencies [12]. This growing tendency to use FIDIC is 
often driven by the fact that contract negotiators are familiar 
with their contractual commitments under the General Con-
ditions, allowing them to focus solely on the modifications 
made in the Particular Conditions [ 13]. Furthermore, uti -
lizing such standardized forms of contract can help reduce 
project risks [11, 14]. Lastly, parties in civil law countries, 
such as Egypt, are often hesitant to adopt the common law 
as the governing law of their contracts, preferring their own 
national laws with which they are more familiar with [6].
Nonetheless, even when FIDIC forms of contracts are 
utilized, each contract form is governed by a different legal 
system [4, 15] that it has to follow to be enforceable [ 16]. 
This can create conflicts when FIDIC’s common law-based 
provisions contradict the Egyptian legal mandates [ 17]; 
thus, parties must carefully assess how local laws impact 
FIDIC contract conditions [18]. While Egypt’s construction 
sector extensively utilizes FIDIC contracts, failure to con -
sider governing law implications can lead to legal disputes 
[19]. Therefore, FIDIC conditions must be customized for 
implementation in different legal environments and tailored 
to align with local statutory principles [ 20]. Hence, clauses 
that may cause conflict between FIDIC and the applicable 
law must be addressed [ 21], as differing interpretations of 
provisions can lead to inconsistencies when using FIDIC 
forms under the Egyptian public law jurisdiction.
Accordingly, a number of studies attempted to address the 
different viewpoints on adapting FIDIC forms of contracts. 
Yet, only a handful of studies dealt with the interpretation 
of FIDIC provisions under Egyptian civil law, and rarely do 
studies deal with FIDIC conditions interpretation under the 
public law in Egypt. For instance, the study conducted by 
Sarie-Eldin’s [22] was one of the first studies to specifically 
address Egyptian public law, discussing the general legal 
applicability of FIDIC as well as some particular topics like 
arbitration and special powers granted to the public entity 
under Egyptian public law.
Moreover, Shafik et al. [23] discussed the application of 
FIDIC 1987 and 1999 under the Egyptian Civil Code (ECC), 
highlighting the conflicts that may arise from such appli -
cation. This study focused on the clauses related to force 
majeure, termination, interest charges, and subcontracting 
to highlight the resemblances and discrepancies between the 
ECC articles and the FIDIC 1999 clauses related to those 
issues and provide recommendations to avoid any conflicts 
as a result of these discrepancies [ 23]. Similarly, Fawzy et 
al. [7] examined how the FIDIC Red Book’s clauses related 
to contract price, delayed payment, and payment mecha -
nisms are applied under the ECL.
Furthermore, a recent study by Abu Helw and Ezeldin 
[24]studied both the contract termination and arbitration 
under ELC and they recommended that future studies cover 
other contract’s aspects to reach a fair and balanced public 
works contract. Most recently, Elshamy et al. [25] assessed 
the similarities and differences between the ECC and FIDIC 
2017 with regards to the conditions in which the contrac -
tor is eligible for time extension and delay damages. Never-
theless, none of the above studies attempted to investigate 
the applicability of FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 under the ELC, 
which is the motivation behind this study.

Goal and objectives:
To address this knowledge gap, this study examined the 
ways of aligning FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses with ELC. 
Through a survey questionnaire, the study first identified 
the three most conflicting clauses between FIDIC (EPC&T) 
1999 and the ELC; then a comparison between the handling 
of these three clauses in both FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 and 
ELC was performed. This was ultimately followed with 
proposed amendments for the contract’s Particular Condi -
tions to ensure better alignment with the ELC and prevent 
future legal disputes.
Research methodology
In this study, a mixed multiphased approach consisting of a 
series of interconnected steps [ 26] was utilized to achieve 
the stated research objective. As demonstrated in Fig. 1, the 
first phase of the study involved a thorough literature review 
to identify the research gap and highlight its significance. 
Next, the second phase consisted of a questionnaire survey 
used to analyze the attitudes and observations of industry 
experts engaged in the Egyptian construction and contract 
administration fields, This phase aimed at extracting the 
major FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses that can cause con -
flicts with ELC. This method was chosen as it is an effec -
tive, standardized, and quick data collection method [ 27] 
that is widely used to gather professional opinions in the 
construction sector [ 28]. Moreover, this method allowed 
the researchers to survey the participants’ prior experi -
ences and examine their perceptions. After identifying the 
major FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses that conflict with ELC, 
the following phase of the study involved analyzing these 
clauses and examining these conflicts through the use of 
case studies from the Egyptian construction industry, then 
provide recommendations on how to mitigate such conflicts 
in the future.
Questionnaire survey
The questionnaire begins with an introduction that outlines 
its purpose, method of analysis, and its significance. After 
the introduction, the survey was divided into four main sec-
tions. The first section collects the participants’ background 
information, including: number of years of experience, and 
professional organization membership. The aim of this sec-
tion is to establish that participants possess the adequate 
expertise to provide insightful and credible responses [ 29]. 
The second section included questions that are aimed at 
assessing the use of the FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 contract 
form as a public works contract in Egypt which was then 
followed by a section aimed at providing recommenda -
tions for the use of FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 in Egypt. Finally, 
the fourth section identifies the implications of conflicts 
between FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses and existing public 
law articles.

Termination
Terminating a contractor’s contract is a serious measure that 
neads to be managed in a conflict-free manner [ 41]. The 
basic principle governing the execution of public works 
contract is similar to the principle prevailing in private law, 
which is the principle of respect for the covenant and the 
implementation of mutual obligations in good faith. How -
ever, in public works contracts, the contractor is held to 
more rigorous and stricter standards. This is due to the fact 
that the public interest in the operation of the public facil -
ity requires a more stringent contractor’s obligations toward 
the employer as it is not permissible to expose the public 
interest to the risk in exchange for the contractor’s interest 
[4].
Termination provisions under FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999
The FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 includes provisions for: 
(1) employer termination due to contractor default; (2) 
employer termination for convenience; (3) contractor ter -
mination due to employer default; (4) termination by either 
party due to force majeure; or (5) release from performance.

Termination provisions under ELC
Under the ELC, public entities possess both manda -
tory and discretionary powers to terminate public works 
contracts; however, the ELC does not explicitly grant con -
tractors the right to terminate such contracts. Mandatory ter-
mination is governed by Article 50 of the ELC and Article 
100 of its executive regulation. It is triggered by contractor 
misconduct, such as fraud, collusion, corruption, or monop-
oly practices, or by bankruptcy and insolvency. In such 
cases, the contract must be terminated, and the contractor is 
barred from future public contracts.
On the other hand, discretionary termination is permitted 
under Article 51 of the ELC and Article 101 of its executive 
regulation if the contractor breaches a substantial provision. 
Yet, the law does not define substantial breaches, leaving 
its determination solely to the public entity. Moreover, any 
decision to terminate at the contractor’s expense must be 
substantiated and formally communicated.
In all cases of termination, the public entity is entitled 
to confiscate the performance bond, deducts penalties and 
losses from amounts due to the contractor, and, if neces -
sary, offsets amounts owed against entitlements from other 
public entities without judicial proceedings, while retaining 
the right to claim outstanding amounts through legal action.

Adjustments for changes in costs concept under FIDIC 
(EPC&T) 1999
Sub-Clause 13.8 of the FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 merely 
states that “if the contract price is to be adjusted for rises or 
falls in the cost of labor, goods, and other inputs to the works, 
the adjustments shall be calculated under the provisions in 
the particular conditions”. Thus, the matter is in the hands of 
the contracting parties to agree on any price increase. This is 
further evidenced by reference to the guidance for the prep-
aration of particular conditions of the FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 
stating that “provisions for adjustment may be required if it 
would be unreasonable for the contractor to bear the risks of 
escalating costs due to inflation”.
Adjustments for changes in costs concept under ELC
Article 47 of the ELC mandates cost adjustments for con-
struction contracts exceeding six months. Adjustments are 
made quarterly based on the producer price indices issued 
by the Central Agency for Public Mobilization and Statis -
tics. This applies from the technical envelope opening date 
or contract signing in direct awards. The adjustment is bind-
ing, and any contrary agreement is void. The public entity 
must specify variable items in the RFP, with contractors 
including relevant coefficients in their technical offers.
Article 97 of the executive regulation outlines conditions 
for applying the cost adjustment formula:
 ● The public entity must define variable items in the RFP; 
otherwise, the tender is canceled.
 ● Contractors must include coefficients representing cost 
elements in their technical offers, or their offers will be 
excluded.
 ● Approved interim payments are made on schedule with-
out waiting for cost adjustments.
 ● Cost adjustments are paid every three months from the 
technical envelope opening date or direct award order.
 ● Payments for cost adjustments must be made within 60 
days of the contractor’s statement submission.
Thus, cost adjustments are obligatory for public works con-
tracts lasting six months or more, and any attempt to fix 
prices by excluding this concept is void under Article 47 of 
the ELC and its executive regulations.

While the ELC does not explicitly address termination 
for convenience, public entities can unilaterally terminate 
contracts for public interest [ 4]. This authority is inherent 
and cannot be waived or restricted. Under this principle, the 
public entity must compensate the contractor for reason -
able costs incurred but not for lost profit. Moreover, since 
the Supreme Administrative Court mandates prior notice 
(Appeal No. 3576), this means that sub-clause 15.5 of 
FIDIC (EPC&T) does not conflict with the ELC.
Termination by contractor due to employer default
The ELC does not explicitly grant contractors the right 
to terminate public works contracts. Contractors must seek 
judicial approval for termination (Appeal No. 1027). How -
ever, since this is not a public policy rule, contracts can 
include termination rights for contractors [4]. Consequently, 
no amendments are needed for FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999.
Termination due to force majeure
The ELC lacks specific provisions on force majeure, 
but the ECC applies, allowing contract termination due to 
impossibility of performance (Appeal No. 689). Article 373 
of the ECC states that obligations terminate if an external 
cause prevents fulfillment. Moreover, Article 159 extends 
this to reciprocal obligations, and Article 664 applies to 
works contracts. Since FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 aligns with 
these principles, Sub-Clause 19.6 can be applied under the 
ELC without issue.
Release from performance
Similar to force majeure, the ELC does not mention 
release from performance, but the ECC provides guidance 
[19, 43]. Articles 373, 664, and 159 state that contracts dis-
solve if performance becomes impossible due to an external 
cause beyond the debtor’s control. Both the ECC and FIDIC 
(EPC&T) 1999 recognize automatic contract rescission in 
such cases, ensuring Sub-Clause 19.7 aligns with the ELC.
Adjustments for changes in costs
The duration of major construction contracts often makes 
their implementation vulnerable to the risks of fluctuat -
ing prices of materials, equipment, and labor, which may 
threaten the ability of contractors to fulfill their obliga -
tions. The practice of fixing contract prices throughout 
the implementation period, regardless of duration and/or 
prevailing market conditions, is an attempt to burden the 
contractor with the risks of price fluctuation while protect -
ing the employer from the consequences of any increase in 
these prices. Nonetheless, this practice may lead to several 
negative impacts, especially for projects with long durations 
[44]. On the other hand, fixed-price contracts might be suit-
able for short-term projects, where the contractor can secure 
necessary supplies immediately after the contract award, or

Hence, this study can has several practical and theorati -
cal contributions. Regarding the former, this study provides 
contract administrators and project parties with a systematic 
framework for adapting FIDIC EPC&T 1999 contracts to 
comply with the ELC. The proposed clause modifications 
offer thourough guidance for drafting the Particular Condi -
tions of Contract that both respect statutory requirements 
while preserving the functional structure of the FIDIC con -
tracts. Accordingly, the findings of this study will help in 
reducing contractual disputes, improving project gover -
nance, and encouraging broader participation in public EPC 
projects in Egypt.

to the construction law and contract management literature 
by extending the analysis of FIDIC contracts into the public 
law–governed contracts in civil law jurisdictions. The ana -
lytical approach adopted in this study provides a framework 
that can be applied to other FIDIC clauses and countries 
with similar legal systems.
 
Conclusion
The application of FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 in public projects 
in Egypt has become increasingly common, especially in 
projects that involves international parties. However, sev -
eral FIDIC (EPC&T) 1999 clauses may conflict with the 
applicable civil laws in various countries. Hence, this study 
investigated the potential conflicts that could arise from 
implementing the provisions for delay damages, termi -
nation, and cost adjustment for changes in cost of FIDIC 
(EPC&T) 1999 under ELC’s respective provisions.
Based on a questionnaire survey and detailed analysis of 
case studies, the study identified delay damages, termina -
tion, and adjustments for changes in costs as the most con -
flict-prone clauses. A comparative analysis of these clauses 
under both FIDIC EPC&T and ELC revealed areas of align-
ment as well as fundamental differences. Accordingly, the 
study proposed a number of amendments to the Particular 
Conditions of Contract to ensure legal enforceability and
