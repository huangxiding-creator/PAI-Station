Balancing 
geopolitics 
and the 
compelling  
story
• Fat tail risks exist during the fog of war, with prospects that the geopolitics could erupt into a 
regional confrontation with global reverberations, not insignificant – miscalculations abound.
• Promisingly, the region’s pragmatism in delicately balancing conflicts with its laser focus on 
the inward-looking economic diversification strategies remains resolute (and credit positive).
• We hold conviction that the region’s giga-scale transformation is still in the early innings.
The Middle 
East’s 
global 
outlier 
status 
remains  
resolute
• Juxtaposed against today’s geopolitical uncertainty is the Middle East’s global outlier status:
1. Short-term and cyclical. Growth remains well-anchored by rapid diversification agendas, 
with debt low, public finances strong, balance of payments robust, balance sheets 
unrivalled, creditworthiness resilient and access to debt capital markets is comfortable.
2. Medium-term and structural. Today’s pace of renewable energy investments is “too 
slow” for net zero, and concurrently investments in fossil fuels is “too low” to make up 
the shortfall. This underinvestment warrants a higher hydrocarbon pricing regime, which 
continues to fortify the firepower of the region’s sovereign wealth funds (SWFs).
3. Long-term and transformational. Game changing shifts in the modus operandi by altering 
the functioning of financial, labour and legal landscapes is yielding tangible returns – a 
testament of the seriousness that regional leaderships are turning aspiration into action.
Four 
themes 
shaping the 
2025 
outlook
• We contextualise what are set to be key themes shaping the Middle East outlook into 2025:
1. APAC – Middle East synergies. Beyond energy – trade, investments and human capital.
2. Saudi Arabia’s “rightsizing” of project spending. Scale backs ease overheating risks.
3. Lower Fed rates and the USD pegs. Negative gearing banks to lower rates to outperform.
4. Burgeoning role of SWFs. Central in supporting vision strategies – ample bankability.

Post US elections – economics | Trump 2.0 and the Middle East
Middle East economic relations with a Trump 2.0 can be examined via three transmission channels – (1) energy (oil and gas) prices; (2) 
the knock-on effects of a strong US dollar for regional pegged currencies, and (3) the outlook for US interest rates.
|  Confidential
 page 12Source: Bloomberg, GCC Ministry of Finances, IMF, MUFG Research; * recently, Trump had endorsed repealing the US’s “double taxation” of                           
its citizens living abroad. It is highly uncertain whether this could be passed, but if it does, it could be another factor that encourages more US citizens to 
consider living abroad in cities such as in the GCC region
Banks / FIs
• GCC banks exhibit one of the strongest gearing to higher rates within the EM space – biggest beneficiaries given their access to sizable non-
interest bearing deposit bases while loan books are principally floating rate in configuration , signalling NIM expansion. Under Trump 2.0 
inflationary policies, this is reinforced. Saudi banks stand out as more resilient relative to UAE / Qatari banks, given their larger non-interest 
bearing deposit bases whilst the loan books are mostly floating – SNB, Al Rajhi and Riyad Bank screen best-in-class.
Consumers
• An easing of regional tensions would benefit key QSR corporates (such as Alamar and Americana). De-escalation could reduce inflationary 
pressures through commodity price decreases, and easing of supply chain constraints (i.e., Red Sea obstruction) would support cheaper 
imports. A stronger US dollar coupled with easing of tensions could also support consumption and outbound travel and tourism. Given 
potential oil price weakness, government focus would be more solidly on non-oil revenues and would increase on inbound tourism.
Healthcare • We see the healthcare sector as broadly unaffected from Trump 2.0. Depending on the rate outlook, we may see high interest costs causing margin pressure in the sector and higher capex intensity. We could see an increased tilt towards buying local for pharma products.
Insurance
• Oil price weakness will likely accelerate greater privatisation of the healthcare ecosystem, where insurance plays a key role. This could enable 
a push towards bridging the enforcement gap in health insurance and the vehicle segments. The government could also encourage newer 
segments, such as life insurance. Investment income would benefit from a more gradual reduction in interest rates.
IT Services
• The sector derives ~50% of its revenue from the government, and as such, oil price weakness and potential pressure on governmental 
budgets may impact the top-line and working capital for corporates. However, a strong focus on efficiency may lead to increased digitalisation 
for the sector. US dollar strength would support the M&A in other markets that can boost internal capabilities of businesses, such as Solutions.
Oil & Gas
• Today, the regional oil and gas entities are driven by global oil macro factors rather than geopolitical events / US election results. The sector 
has low leverage on average and is neutral to any changes in rates outlook. The same applies to certain utilities under similar circumstances.
Real Estate
• The possible impact on the GCC real estate could be far-reaching. Possibly the most overarching impact might be from Trump-driven policies 
leading to higher US inflationary pressure and therefore prolonged higher interest rates globally. In essence, indirect (listed) real estate is 
negatively correlated to bond yields. For the GCC region, a strengthening US dollar translates to a relatively higher US dollar value for GCC 
property, and the mortgage market will forgo any near-term stimulus that it may have got from lower rates*.
Telecoms
• Rather defensive and among the least susceptible to geopolitical tensions. US dollar strength could affect corporates with global exposure, 
especially those in Africa and some parts of Asia. Single country operators are relatively insulated from such volatility. Any changes in the global 
rates outlook post-elections could impact expected dividend yield or discount rates for the sector, given its general perception as a yield proxy.
Transport
• If we were to see a normalisation of the geopolitical backdrop, this could imply that the temporary spike in air cargo, due to concerns around 
trade through the Red Sea, would fade. Lower oil prices could support inbound and outbound tourism in the GCC region. Airlines would be 
beneficiaries from lower oil and jet fuel prices in the short term.

• Saudi Arabia’s calculation is nuanced. With public debt of ~25% of GDP sitting well below EM peers, central bank foreign assets the highest in the region and 
strong progress with divestment giving the authorities ready access to funding, the Kingdom has ample room for manoeuvre. Yet, the Kingdom is entering the 
most capital-intensive phase of Vision 2030, that is driving spending gains ahead of increases in non-oil revenues. The centrality of the investment goals suggests

• We are comfortable with Qatar’s capacity to manage even a protracted period of energy price weakness . This stems from the scale of the hydrocarbon wealth

of per capita GDP. With Qatar having completed the bulk of its capital-intensive infrastructure build-out pre-2022 FIFA World Cup, there is less upward pressure on 
public spending, mitigating concerns over limited non-energy exports. Limited progress on non-hydrocarbon growth is an area of focus.
• Though Oman lacks hydrocarbon wealth of the scale of some of its peers, it has delivered a marked improvement in its cyclical and structural capacity to 
manage oil price volatility. The gains have been policy led, focused on public debt reduction, improved spending management, and expansion of non-oil revenues 
that has seen the government introduce VAT and prepare to implement the region’s first ever personal income tax. This has driven the breakeven oil price below 
the regional average and cut public debt by more than 30ppt of GDP which, combined with consolidation of sovereign wealth assets, has enhanced the capacity to 
manage strain. Yet, the external account has seen more limited gains, reflecting the reliance on imported goods and labour.
• Kuwait remains the least diversified of the regional energy exporters, with shifts in the oil price leading to pronounced moves that saw the budget balance move 
by more than 40ppt of GDP between 2020 and 2022. Like Qatar, Kuwait has yet to introduce VAT, but spending continues to gain, driven by increases in public 
wages and transfers, while export volumes are subject to OPEC+ constraints. Kuwait’s large stock of sovereign wealth and low levels of debt provide 
reassurance and serve to keep its credit rating strong. However, there is currently no law in place to allow the government to borrow, while only the General 
Reserve Fund (rather than the larger Future Generation Fund) is accessible to meet financing needs, constraining the support balance sheet strength can offer. 
• Bahrain has a diversified economic base, but its oil output falls short of its peers in both in absolute and per capita terms. It has a multi-year fiscal consolidation 
plan that seen VAT double VAT to 10% in 2022 with plans for a new corporate tax. Yet, spending has proved testing to curb, while the scale of the debt stock has 
put debt servicing on an upward path, further narrowing the room for manoeuvre. This has seen the budget run a deficit every year since 2009, lifting public debt 
north of 100% of GDP. Regional support continues, but it is not clear in what scale, scope or timeframe this will flow going forward.

Middle East risks | optimism frames our view but the cycle is moderating
The cycle is moderating as it matures, and policy tests are turning acute. The divergence in prospects is also apparent. We c ontextualise 
five dimensions to our concerns as to why unresolved policy rigidities alongside exogenous factors may begin to bite.
• Uncertainties hanging over a weak and uneven global growth outlook remain top of mind – notably close links to a tepid China.
• Alongside the headwinds to trade, the global backdrop continues to complicate the region’s FDI access – it is hard to gauge quantums and whilst the 
initial shock of the Gaza conflict has ebbed, regional geopolitics remains front and centre of attention, with some level of FDI likely been delayed, notably 
those linked with trade (sea disruptions). Yet for now, there is a clear recognition of compartmentalising risks from MENA ex. GCC HY (explicitly impacted 
from the conflict, such as Lebanon and Iran) and core GCC IG credits (Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar and Kuwait).
• For all the comfort of GCC US dollar pegs, the monetary system has brought cyclical headwinds which may be slow to reverse. GCC currencies have 
appreciated on a trade-weighted real effective exchange rate (REER) basis, as the US dollar has strengthened – this has been offset to some degree by 
the negative inflation differential, but further US dollar appreciation creates regional headwinds as it seeks to attract FDI and diversify its non-oil base*.
• Also, while spot FX prices have held firm, previous periods of lower oil prices have seen onshore and offshore investors start to question the peg as 
deficits built and reserves dropped, triggering pronounced moves in FX forward rates that have disrupted market function and deepened uncertainty.
• The underlying fiscal and external account shifts that come with the non-oil growth cycle are unlikely to generate strain provided oil earnings remain 
stable – and we have conviction in our long-standing supercycle thesis of higher-for-longer oil prices anchored on structural underinvestment. 
• As such, medium-term risks would appear to be to the upside, signalling a strong position that boosts options for capex funding. However, should a 
global hard landing transpire and/or any unforeseen bearish shock and/or a period of weak oil prices into 2025, fragilities (albeit not immediate) will 
materialise, even for economies with low breakeven oil prices.
• Middle East sovereigns have loosened fiscal policy, drawing on public balance sheets to support large-scale capex. With budget prospects still strong and 
public debt low, this is not a concern, however, the strong overall outturn masks an underlying trend within which public spending is rising more rapidly 
than non-oil revenues, pushing the non-oil deficit higher.
• This shift is offset to some degree by strong growth in the non-oil economy, though, the widening of the shortfall in nominal terms, increases the sum 
that must be covered by oil receipts, thus pushing breakeven oil prices higher, especially in Saudi Arabia**.
• From a funding perspective, should oil prices drop and revenues fall, government deposit growth can go into reverse as energy-related entities draw on 
savings for funding. In tandem, governments’ need for additional funding to cover larger fiscal shortfalls sees them borrow more, both through 
increases in demand for bank credit, and higher domestic debt issuance. Thus, a lower oil price environment feeds into large changes in the state 
sector’s net claims on the banks, tightening liquidity, pushing the private sector to the margins and boosting credit spreads as it competes for funding . 
• The shift in fortune can be especially marked given how small regional banking systems are, and the limited options for long term funding – no Middle 
East economy has yet a significant local currency corporate debt market, for example, and venture capital finance remains in its infancy. 
• Granted, foreign ownership of domestic debt instruments is low, which guards against capital outflows and risk re-pricing that might otherwise 
accompany lower oil prices – but the limited ties between domestic debt markets and foreign savings denies the region a funding channel available in 
major EMs, and is only partly countered by increased foreign liabilities in the domestic banking system .

How fast are renewable energy projects progressing in Saudi Arabia?
• Several projects have moved into the construction phase since 2023, or are 
currently in permitting stages, and project tendering has accelerated. As of H1 
2024, ~8.4GW in solar PV capacity under construction, of which ~6.6GW pertains 
to the PIF/ACWA programme, with 3 new additional projects (total capacity of 
5.5GW) recently announced. Combined, these account for ~13.6GW in identified 
capacity in the PIF/ACWA pipeline, exceeding the initial target of 11.8GW by 
end-2024.
Beyond solar, wind, hydrogen and carbon capture are also a focus for renewables
• For wind projects, Saudi has so far brought online only one IPP project under the 
National Renewable Energy Programme (NREP), the 400MW Dumat Al Jandal
project, which came on stream in 2022 and was connected to the electricity grid 
last year. Total identified capacity for wind projects to 4.3GW (~15% of total), 
vs. 25.8GW in solar PV projects (~85%).

hydrogen opportunity, benefiting from its rich natural resources, existing 
infrastructure, low-cost renewable power generation and attractive geographic 
location for export routes. Progress continues with the NEOM Green Hydrogen 
Company, alongside ACWA Power and Air Products, is developing one of the

journey. Saudi is embarking on massive growth ambitions on the CCS front with 
an aim to increase capacity from ~1mtpa to ~10mtpa by 2030 and 44mtpa by

• Bahrain’s troubled prospects may be further exacerbated by a period of softer oil prices, focusing attention on vulnerabiliti es in its 
domestic and external accounts. 
• The absence of timely fiscal data makes it difficult to gauge how much pressure the budget faces, but we estimate the shortfa ll will 
exceed 5% of GDP this year. This would be the highest shortfall across the Middle East energy exporters, but data showing cen tral 
bank claims on government increased by the equivalent of 6.5% of GDP over the first three-quarters of 2024 signals risks are to the 
upside going forward.
• The firm commitment to fiscal rebalancing is intact, and falling oil revenues may prompt an acceleration on expenditure 
rationalisation as well as potential further tax hikes, in additional to the new corporation tax set to take effect in 2025.
• Having said that, we are cognisant that five years since the Fiscal Balance Programme (FBP) was announced, expenditure has 
proved tough to control, with outlays running 6% ahead of budget in 2023. Thus, we now see the deficit pushing north of 7% of
GDP in 2025-26, as oil earnings likely soften, with funding pressures driven higher by the large refinancing obligations the sovereign 
needs to confront in the coming years. The large borrowing need is expect to take public debt to over 127% of GDP.
• External account pressures are less acute, with the current account set to register a fourth consecutive surplus this year.
• However, central bank foreign reserves have remained low despite the strong run of external surpluses – at USD4.3bn in August 
2024 – an increase of only USD300m since January 2024, with the stock equating to just two and a half months of import cover – a
low sum for an oil dependent economy operating a US dollar peg – which is likely to fall further if oil receipts soften. 
• In contrast to others in the Middle East, there are only limited foreign assets held outside of the central bank by Mumtalakat.
• Bahrain’s unwavering support from its regional neighbours and other allies remains resolute, especially if cyclical pressures and 
structural weaknesses intensify. However, it is not clear in what scale, scope or timeframe this will flow going forward.
• Though policy conditionality in exchange of financial support may tighten, the focus would remain on safeguarding stability. 
• Previous regional support programmes have also buttressed capital projects in the country that remain an critical driver of growth. 
Demand from fast-growing Saudi Arabia is also an important dimension of corporate activity, but with sentiment waning and 
consumer spending moderating, we see growth remaining slightly below average at ~3%.

• Given the sheer complexity of the challenges at hand, it is too soon to gauge the changes in governing structure will have on policy 
execution. Encouragingly, there have been some signals of progress, including a fresh cabinet reshuffle in August 2024 that s aw the 
appointment of a new Finance Minister. The appointment of a new crown prince in June without reference to the suspended 
parliament also points to a changed approach to sensitive policy issues. For the time being, we still wait to see the contours of the 
broad reform agenda, and in particular how quickly fiscal priorities will be adjusted, and what steps government will take on
investment as well as structural reforms to start to close the gap that has emerged between Kuwait and its peers.
• GDP contracted by 2.7% y/y in Q1 2024 (latest available data) – the weakest outturn in the region. While the contraction primarily 
reflected OPEC-led production cuts, the non-oil sector remains flat. This tepidness is corresponded across high frequency data, with 
the latest PMI readings pointing to stagnation, led by feeble new orders and weak corporate sentiment.
• Meanwhile, full-year fiscal data show a deficit of 3.1% of GDP for 2023/24, a 15ppt of GDP annualised deterioration led by spending 
on wages and subsidies. With this spending pattern rigid to amend swiftly, the deficit is set to widen in 2025-26, if oil income
(comprising ~90% of government revenues) subsides.
• Against the backdrop of policy bottlenecks and weak data readings, is an unprecedented quantum of wealth buffers. The current
account recorded a surplus of ~30% of GDP in Q1 2024 (annualised), allowing the state to add to a stock of foreign assets tha t the 
Kuwait Investment Authority (KIA) is now estimated at USD980bn*. 
• These foreign assets provide a pool of capital and source of income that could be brought onshore, and a buffer against  external 
shock. They are less accessible for fiscal funding, however, and with a new debt law still not in place, fiscal strains would build if oil 
prices trend sharply lower, as they did over 2020-21.

• Oman’s rebalancing since the new Sultan took office in January 2020, has been nothing short of outstanding. The highly succes sful 
fiscal adjustment programme that has dominated Omani policymaking over the past four years has continued to deliver returns, 
materially enhancing the sovereign’s capacity to weather periods of oil price weakness.
• These broad strides drove S&P’s decision in September 2024 to return Oman’s credit rating to the investment -grade status it  lost 
in March 2019 – a key policy focus that serves as a marker of progress made towards reducing the economy’s dependence on 
hydrocarbons.
• Fiscal gains accentuate the sheer velocity of the gains, with the budget set to print its third consecutive year of surplus i n 2024 – a 
marked turnaround on the deficits that averaged in double-digits between 2014-21. What is most impressive is that the 
rebalancing has been led by expenditure rationalisation and a determined liability management strategy that has witnessed the
sovereign repay a further USD3bn in external borrowing this year. This has reduced public debt to below 40% of GDP, and put 
interest expenditure on a downward path*. The push to diversify the state’s income streams also retains momentum, with non -oil 
revenue gains holding as the country prepares to introduce the first income tax across Middle East exporters.
• The external account has seen more limited gains, reflecting the intensity of the economy’s reliance on imported goods and la bour 
and modest progress with broader diversification plans.
• Growth in tourism receipts appears to be fading while the 20% increase in the expatriate population over the past two years p oints 
to potentially strong growth in remittance outflows. 
• Central Bank of Oman (CBO) reserve buffers have been broadly flat so far in 2024 (at around five months’ import cover), thoug h we 
look for FX accumulation to resume as the debt repayment drive winds down.
• The non-oil economy has been the key engine of growth this year, rising by a significant 4.2% y/y pace in H1 2024, owing to 
strengthening industrial and service sector activity. This strong print has been partly offset by the impact of OPEC+ product ion cuts 
that have weighed on the oil sector, keeping headline GDP ~1% this year. Meanwhile, slowing labour market and tourism sector 
data signal momentum may have waned into Q3 2024. 
• Expectations of firm non-oil activity gaining momentum, alongside the gradual unwinding of OPEC+ production cuts is likely to 
support headline GDP growth back towards ~3-4% in 2025-26.

• Saudi Arabia is entering the most capital-intensive phase of Vision 2030, which is driving gains in spending well ahead of increases 
in non-oil revenues. This has led the non-oil fiscal deficit to a record high in Q2 2024, with the outsize role Saudi Arabia plays in 
OPEC+ production cuts driving upward a breakeven oil price that could exceed USD100/b in 2025 when factoring in off -balance

• Lower oil revenues and slower FDI progress has spurred a reprioritisation in projects with scale backs a testament that the 
economy is maturing. Although the authorities have not confirmed changes in project spending and/or any changes to the Nation al 
Investment Strategy, the Minister of Economy has stated that the pace of implementation of the projects may be recalibrated t o 
optimise performance. What is clear is that the sheer velocity of the announced projects is beyond the capacity of the econom y to 
implement without overheating, as well as ensuring costs are contained and effective sequence management. How the authorities
deliver such large quantum's of capex without crowding out the private sector is also a focus.
• Vision 2030 project implementation concerns are not new, but they do appear to be gaining traction, and there seemingly is a 
policy pivot within various public institutions towards project “rightsizing” and countering excess. This, together with the ample 
room for manoeuvre the Kingdom’s wealth provides, and low levels of debt, offers credit comfort. For now, we do not expect fi scal 
shortfalls to trigger a meaningful adjustment in spending priorities – a view supported by plans laid out in the preliminary 2025 
budget. Yet, given the urgency with which the development work is being pursued, any oil revenue weakness may require difficu lt 
decisions between accelerating balancing sheet drawdown, rising debt and/or recalibrating investment ambitions.

household consumption, service sector exports and an acceleration in investment spending. Strong PMI prints suggest momentum 
remains expansionary, reinforcing that the geopolitical backdrop has not taken a toll on corporate confidence – a view supported
by firm capital market performance. Oil production cuts will offset the impact of non-oil gains on overall GDP in 2024, leaving 
headline growth at 1.5%. Though, provided production normalises as we expect in 2025, overall GDP growth should rise to ~4 -5% 
between 2025-26, pushing nominal GDP to USD1.2tn by 2026 – an 80% increase in a decade.

• The UAE’s economic outlook remains best placed across the Middle East. The positive view in part reflects the scale of the wealth 
the UAE commands, with high savings and low debt, providing buffers against external shocks. The bulk of that balance sheet 
strength sits with oil-rich Abu Dhabi, but Dubai has also paid down debt over the past five years and set asset sales in train, all in 
the context of ongoing institutional reform. The government has no financing needs over the next two years, given healthy 
surpluses as well as significant cash excess. It is likely to continue to finance any upcoming bond maturities from these, although 
the possibility of some issuance for debt management purposes is not ruled out entirely.
• Economic growth prospects for the UAE are robust, which we expect to average ~5% between 2025 -26. The growth drivers are 
broad, underpinned by ongoing population growth and rapid gains in service sector exports, led by expatriate inflows, travel and
tourism that have so far not been impacted by conflict in the broader region. 
• We also look for investment spending to remain firm as both Abu Dhabi and Dubai continue to pursue their medium-term 
development programmes, and federal infrastructure is upgraded.
• High frequency data points to expansion. The PMI reading, remains well in expansionary territory, driven by rising new orders, 
output and employment. Tourist arrival numbers hit a record high in H1 2024, and airport traffic has pushed well above pre -COVID
levels. Project data signal that the value of projects planned and underway rising, with new contracts being signed in construction, 
transport and hydrocarbons. Lending to the private sector has also gained and Fed-driven monetary easing is set to deliver a more 
marked credit impulse in the UAE than elsewhere across Middle East US dollar pegged countries, given strong liquidity conditi ons
and positive sentiment.
• While headline inflation has settled at ~3%, ongoing double-digit rises in rents suggest underlying pressures may be more 
pronounced. Sharp rises in property prices are a risk and while lending to the real estate sector is less marked than during previous 
boom periods, off-plan purchases are gaining. As the most open and trade-orientated economy in the region, the UAE is more 
exposed than its peers to any further downturn in global growth, particularly in China and India. The risk of further escalation in the 
regional conflict is also a risk to its development ambitions and FDI flows, though in the past we note that deepening unrest
elsewhere in the Middle East has boosted the appeal of the open and stable UAE.
