Abstract
This article examines China’s environmental diplomacy through the Green Silk Road 
(GSR), a relatively recent yet strategically significant aspect of the Belt and Road 
Initiative (BRI). While existing literature has extensively analysed the economic and 
geopolitical dimensions of the BRI, the environmental narrative remains underex-
plored, especially concerning China’s international role. Drawing on Role Theory 
and Moral Realism, this study investigates how China’s efforts to “green” the BRI 
serve as a mechanism of role adjustment, aligning its self-perception with changing 
international expectations of responsible power conduct. Using a primarily qualita-
tive methodology that combines content analysis, expert interviews, and a targeted 
online survey, the article explores the GSR’s impact on China’s global image and its 
positioning as an emerging environmental leader. Focusing on three regions—Cen-
tral Asia, Latin America, and Europe—the analysis shows how environmental pro-
jects influence perceptions of China’s legitimacy and leadership. Although the GSR 
seems to enhance China’s international image, scepticism remains about the sincer -
ity of its environmental commitments. The study argues that the GSR marks both a 
strategic and normative shift in China’s foreign policy, with consequences for global 
environmental governance and the changing political landscape in the Asia–Pacific 
and beyond.

ping in 2013, a substantial body of literature has emerged on the subject (Khanal 
& Zhang 2024). Some studies examine the BRI in broader terms, considering its

international impacts and effects, as well as its contribution to China’s development 
and its assertion on the global stage (An & Wang 2024; Du 2025; Hu 2025; Wong & 
Downes 2024). Others focus on issues of bilateral or regional cooperation within the 
BRI framework (Morales Ruvalcaba 2025; Ullah & Xinlei 2025).
Regarding its corridors, the majority of the literature concentrates on the Silk 
Road Economic Belt (Chen et  al. 2021; Dave & Kobayashi 2018; Duarte et  al. 
2023), the 21st Century Maritime Silk Road (Billé et  al. 2022; Ferreira-Pereira 
et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2023), and, since 2015, the Digital Silk Road (Parzyan 2021; 
Sahakyan 2024; Silva & Duarte 2025). The Green Silk Road (GSR), launched in 
2017, has received less attention in academic literature. The exceptions include the 
works of Ferguson (2021), Mardell (2023), Nitza-Makowska, Longhurst, and Skiert-
Andrzejuk (2024).
From the very beginning of the BRI, scholars have voiced concerns about its envi-
ronmental implications. Ascensão et al. (2018) warned that the expansion of trans-
portation networks would likely result in habitat loss, resource overexploitation, and 
landscape degradation. Echoing these concerns, the World Wildlife Fund (WWF) 
in 2017 found that BRI corridors could potentially impact all protected areas they 
cover, signalling a clear risk of severe negative environmental impacts from infra-
structure development. Many scholars remained sceptical of China’s real environ-
mental motives even after the launch of the Green BRI, arguing that the initiative 
still prioritises economic growth over ecological preservation, particularly in regions 
with weak environmental regulations (Nurgozhayeva 2024; Vidal 2022; Zhou et al. 
2018). The director of the Centre for Tropical Environmental and Sustainability Sci-
ence at James Cook University, William Laurance, emphasised that ‘BRI will still 
have a major impact on ecosystems: China has said that it will be low-carbon, green 
and sustainable, but it is anything but that’(Vidal 2022). His opinion is supported 
by Lihuan Zhou et al. (2018) recalling the Chinese government initial promises to 
embed environmentally sustainable, or green, strategies within the BRI, but his anal-
ysis revealed that between 2014 and 2017, the bulk of bank loans and cross-border 
investments in the energy and transportation sectors were primarily directed towards 
fossil fuel projects. In this context, Qingge Geng and Kevin Lo (2023) noticed that 
the Green BRI holds a symbolic function, especially in defending its reputation as 
a sustainable, win–win project, which currently outweighs its practical contribution 
to environmental protection. On the other hand, several scholars find positive envi-
ronmental dynamics in China’s GSR (Chin et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022; Xu 2023), 
empirically highlighting that the BRI has effectively promoted the progress of green 
technology in the host countries, which contributes to the development of a green 
economy (Liu et al. 2022).
Hence, while existing literature on the Green BRI predominantly focuses on its 
(im)practical implementation, the present paper will not assess the rhetoric or core 
practices of the initiative. Instead, it will examine how setting the goal of sustainabil-
ity and ‘going green’ regardless of its effectiveness or alignment with official green 
objectives—has influenced the conception of China’s international role. Through 
this article, we argue that, after decades of massive pollution, China has recognised 
that the traditional role conception of achieving progress at any cost to nature has 
proven disastrous, thus leading to a role adjustment. Through its green projects,

China engages in a process of role adjustment, not only meeting but also redefining 
international expectations of what a major power’s role should entail in the global 
fight against climate change. This approach suggests that the GSR is less about uni-
lateral role imposition and more about a multilateral process where China attempts 
to establish itself as a ‘Green Great Power’ by legitimising and reinforcing its posi-
tion through mutual recognition and cooperation with other states. The analysis cov-
ers the period from the launch of the GSR in 2017 to China’s second white paper on 
‘Green Development in the New Era’, published in 2023. As such, the research will 
be guided by the following question: To what extent does the GSR contribute to (re)
adjusting China’s role conception to better meet international expectations?
While addressing China’s domestic environmental degradation, this paper identi-
fies three main motives underlying China’s shift towards ‘greening’: the intention to 
become a ‘Green Great Power’ (Yeophantong & Goh 2022) and the need to counter 
external criticism of being a major polluter. Additionally, this paper examines how 
the GSR contributes to China’s international image, focusing particularly on Central 
Asia, Latin America, and Europe as case studies. The present paper identifies a gap 
in the literature which presents an opportunity for further research. Existing studies 
have focused on the renewable energy dependencies that China creates in various 
regions (Bertram 2023; Ferguson 2021; Mardell 2023; Rodenbiker 2023; Sharifli 
2024). However, they have overlooked the significance of the GSR in enhancing the 
conception of China’s role as an environmental leader, except Hilton (2024).
To support our argument and answer the research question, we have developed 
an eclectic theoretical framework based on the premises of Role Theory and Moral 
Realism, while also incorporating elements related to Environmental Diplomacy and 
Global Governance. Role Theory (Duarte et al. 2024; Harnisch et al. 2011) posits 
that social systems are structured around and guided by roles. This perspective helps 
us understand the evolution and adjustments in China’s international role. However, 
to justify our argument that the GSR results from a role adjustment driven by Bei-
jing’s perception that, to assert itself as a major power, it would need to align with 
international environmental expectations, we must adopt a broader framework. This 
is where Moral Realism (Yan 2016, 2019) comes into play, a theoretical lens which 
holds that a leading power—and any state aspiring to such status—must demonstrate 
responsibility and meet the demands of other international actors. Environmental 
Diplomacy (Ali & Vladich 2016; Broadhurst & Ledgerwood 1998; Orsini 2020), 
in turn, acts as the connector, underpinning the premise that the role adjustment, 
driven by external expectations and international demands, led to the creation of the 
GSR. This initiative serves as a tool of environmental diplomacy, aiming to project 
China as a responsible environmental power. In doing so, it enhances China’s promi-
nence in global governance and paves the way for its emergence as a leading power.
Regarding the methodological framework, this article employs a qualitative meth-
odology. This choice is driven by the need to analyse complex dynamics composed 
of various variables, as well as our aim to interpret them through an established 
theoretical framework. Regarding data collection and analysis techniques, this arti-
cle employs a literature review and interpretative textual analysis. The data includes 
secondary sources, such as reports from think tanks and key academic literature, as 
well as primary sources, with a focus on official Chinese government documents,

news articles, and semi-structured, pre-selected interviews. Additionally, an online 
survey (see Sects. Research Methods and Results and Analysis of Survey Data) will

findings.
2  Connecting the Dots: Intersecting Role Theory, Moral Realism, 
and Environmental Diplomacy in the Study of the BRI
To analyse how the evolution of the BRI and the integration of a green corridor 
translate into an adaptation of the conception of China’s international role, we must 
first establish a robust theoretical framework. This section undertakes that task by 
proposing a framework that integrates role theory, moral realism, and environmental 
diplomacy. While role theory allows us to interpret the adaptation of China’s foreign 
policy (encapsulated in the maxim ‘going green’), Environmental Diplomacy and 
Moral Realism enable us to explain how this adaptation reflects the perception, on 
the Chinese agency’s part, that the environmental responsibility demands from the 
international community must be adhered to for China to become a leading power.
The choice of role theory as a conceptual lens is due to its potential to explain 
why China embarked on the ‘greening’ of the BRI and pursued proactive environ-
mental diplomacy. Specifically, China’s role adaptation from a heavy polluter to a 
‘Green Great Power’ in the making will be assessed using the main variables of 
role theory: role conception, role performance, role expectations, and role adapta-
tion (Duarte et  al. 2024; Harnisch et  al. 2011). In this sense, role conception can 
be defined as an actor’s perception of their position in the international system vis-
à-vis others (ibid.). Xi Jinping’s China perceives itself as a great power engaged in 
‘peaceful development’, wherein environmental sustainability and economic growth 
must be in strict harmony (Duarte et al. 2024; Wehner & Thies 2021).
Another component of role theory is role expectation, defined as the ‘appropri-
ate behaviour’ that an audience anticipates from a specific actor. In this case, it 
can be argued that the international community has encouraged China to gradu-
ally adopt a more responsible role in global governance, particularly concern-
ing environmental issues. This indeed corresponds to another component of 
role theory: role adaptation, which encompasses ‘the changes of strategies and 
instruments in performing a role’ (Harnisch et al. 2011). In this context, China’s 
proactive environmental diplomacy, alongside the GSR as its instrument, can be 
viewed as China’s response to the role expectations established by both its domes-
tic audience and the international community. These entities have urged Beijing 
to assume a more active and responsible role in global environmental initiatives. 
The final component of the role theory is the role performance, meaning the 
actual state behaviour. As noticed by the Chinese political scientist Yan Xuetong

(2014), Beijing’s stance has evolved from ‘keeping a low profile to striving for

The pertinence of applying role theory to the study of BRI is connected to its 
unique approach to roles. Since the BRI is Xi Jinping’s signature project, it is an 
extraordinary domestic and foreign policy tool that reflects the Chinese leader’s con-
ception of China’s role within global governance. Now, the gap between the kind 
of player that China would like to become in the green field (role conception) and 
the actual behaviour (role performance) that Beijing exhibits on the ground may not 
always coincide with the projected goals. This is the case, as later evidenced by the 
reduced outputs that have so far emerged from the GSR. Nevertheless, one should 
acknowledge that Chinese endeavours in going green show a will of role adapta-
tion, from a bystander actor to a more dynamic one in terms of adopting more sus-
tainable policies. Role theory seems, again, to be an interesting approach to analyse 
that shift, as it conceptually cares about adaptation whenever the means/policies no 
longer seem to be effective in achieving one’s goals. This is why China realised that 
turning green would not only prove to benefit the country and the global environ-
ment, it would simultaneously help improve China’s image abroad.
This shift in Chinese international behaviour is better understood when the theo-
retical framework is expanded to include some elements of Moral Realism. Moral 
Realism is a theoretical approach within the field of International Relations pro-
posed by the Chinese scholar Yan Xuetong. This approach is based on realist prem-
ises, namely that the international system is inherently anarchic and that states act 
according to their national interest in a logic of competition and power struggle (Yan 
2016; Zhang 2012).
However, by reintroducing the moral dimension often overlooked by many real-
ists, Yan (2016, 2019) argues that states’ behaviour, particularly major powers, 
should be analysed through the lens of morality, considering it a crucial factor in a 
state’s rise and decline within the international system (Yan 2016).
Moral Realism also highlights the role of political leadership and leaders’ percep-
tions, similarly to role theory. However, for Moral Realism perceptions matter not in 
the sense that they redefine the national interest, but rather in that they influence the 
chosen means through which those interests are achieved (Yan 2016).
In practice, Moral Realism suggests that for a state to rise and establish itself as 
a leading power, it must possess international credibility, which, in turn, depends on 
morality—that is, its ability to act benevolently and responsibly (Silva 2025; Yan 
2019; Zhang 2012). A leading power is thus expected to act responsibly and ‘lead 
by example’; however, when it abandons this moral action, it risks losing credibility, 
thereby undermining its status within the international system (Yan 2016, 2019).
Moral Realism thus enables a broader perspective on China’s role adaptation. 
Recognising that its credibility increasingly depended on aligning with the envi-
ronmental demands and expectations of the international community—and acting 
as a responsible power—China was compelled to adapt or risk seeing its interna-
tional status undermined. At the same time, by undertaking this adaptation, China 
reinforces its credibility, strengthening its prospects of assuming a leading power

position, particularly at a time when the United States appears to be wavering in its 
international agenda.
While role theory and moral realism provide essential frameworks for understand-
ing and interpreting the motivations and objectives underlying the GSR—particu-
larly the argument concerning the adaptation of China’s international role—environ-
mental diplomacy, on the one hand, clarifies how this role adaptation materialises 
in practice and, on the other, illustrates how environmental awareness contributes to 
enhancing China’s global image. In this context, it is pertinent to introduce the con-
cept of the Green Great Power (or Environmental Great Power).
This concept is closely linked to the notion of environmental power—that is, a 
state’s capacity to influence the environment and international relations within the 
environmental domain, whether positively or negatively (Buzan & Falkner 2022). 
In this sense, a Green Great Power refers to a state that occupies a dominant envi-
ronmental position and possesses an enhanced capacity to shape the international 
environmental agenda (Buzan & Falkner 2022).
Environmental diplomacy, in turn, is a form of diplomacy associated with nego-
tiations between international actors on issues related to the environment and envi-
ronmental governance (Ali & Vladich 2016; Broadhurst & Ledgerwood 1998). As 
Orsini (2020) clarifies, although it is traditionally understood as diplomacy dealing 
exclusively with environmental matters, in practice, environmental diplomacy also 
encompasses issues ranging from trade to security.
The fundamental pillars of international environmental regulations include (1) 
the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) of 1992, which 
distinguishes obligations between developed and developing nations; (2) the Kyoto 
Protocol of 1997, recognised as the first legally binding climate change agreement; 
(3) the Copenhagen Accord of 2009, which resulted in non-binding political state-
ments; and (4) the 2015 Paris Agreement, known as the world’s first global climate 
pact with legally binding commitments for all participating countries.
Thus, environmental diplomacy and its connections with climate governance are, 
essential to understand China’s moves. In fact, China is a latecomer in global envi-
ronmental governance. Until the Copenhagen Summit in 2009, China participated 
in climate change negotiations through the Group of 77 (also called G77). 1 Within 
this coalition, China promoted a unified negotiating position, arguing that much of 
the environmental degradation stemmed from human activities in developed nations 
(UN Environment Programme, 2019).  As a result, developed countries should bear 
greater responsibility for addressing these challenges (Xinhua 2023b). In the after -
math of the Copenhagen Summit, China also became a founding member of BASIC 
(Brazil, South Africa, India, and China), a coalition of rapidly developing countries 
aimed at enhancing their influence on global environmental issues.
Notwithstanding, China’s rapid modernisation heightened international pres-
sure and public concerns regarding Beijing’s limited role in environmental issues.

In attempts to adapt to the expected ‘responsible stakeholder’ role (Nitza-

warming initiative with the release of the National Climate Change Program in 
2007 (National Development and Reform Commission of the PRC 2007). This

adopting a flexible approach to address climate change. Subsequently, by 2012, the 
Chinese Communist Party incorporated ‘the establishment of an ecological civili-

Shoaib (2024), Environmental Diplomacy is often viewed as a form of soft power, 
and in this realm, China has achieved notable success (Duarte & Ferreira-Pereira 
2022; Shoaib 2024). Not surprisingly, China has improved its international percep-
tion as indicated by the Soft Power 30 surveys from 2015 to 2019 (the latest year 
available in the survey), in which its score rose from 30 positions in 2015 to 25 in 
2017, and then in 2019 China reached its highest ever score of 24.
China’s move from the role conception of a discreet player to the one of agenda-
setters in global environmental politics has been predominantly boosted by the BRI 
launched in September 2013 (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of PRC 2013). To align 
the international community’s role expectations towards the BRI, which initially 
perceived it as the most carbon-emitting project (Chaudhury 2023; Chiu 2022), 
Brazilian scholar Diego Pautasso 2 informs that ‘Beijing understood the need to 
adopt an environmentally-friendly approach’.3 Therefore, since 2015, when 195 UN 
member states adopted the 2030 Sustainable Development Agenda, Beijing seized 
the opportunity to incorporate the Sustainable Development Goals into the BRI 
to cope with the external criticism whilst attempting to increase the attractiveness 
of the BRI (Duarte & Ferreira-Pereira 2022; Eurasian Rail Alliance Index 2023; 
Nitza-Makowska et al. 2024; D. Pautasso, personal communication, 19 April 2024). 
Unsurprisingly, the green and low-carbon developments were articulated in the BRI 
road map 2015 (He 2020). Furthermore, China’s President Xi Jinping conceptual-
ised the ‘greening’ of the BRI by announcing the launch of the Green Belt and Road 
Initiative in 2017 during the first Belt and Road Forum on May 14–15, 2024 (SCO 
Environment Information Sharing Platform 2017). In this regard, the Joint Commu-
nique of the Leaders’ Roundtable of the Belt and Road Forum in 2017 emphasised 
the importance of both ‘environmental sustainability of projects’ and ‘promoting 
high environmental standards’ (ibid.).

3  Research Methods
This section outlines the research design adopted throughout our analysis. In doing 
so, we seek to make explicit the methodology employed, as well as the techniques 
used for data collection and analysis, which enabled us to reach our findings and 
address the research question.
This study adopts a predominantly qualitative methodology. This choice is jus-
tified by the need to examine complex dynamics, comprising multiple variables, 
which require a subjective and interpretative approach. Moreover, the interpretative 
nature of the study, dictated by the selected theoretical framework, necessitates this 
type of analysis. Accordingly, a qualitative approach allows us to explore and inter -
pret the phenomenon under investigation in ways that a quantitative approach would 
not permit.
The study is structured in three stages. In the first stage, we conduct a qualitative 
analysis and interpretation of the phenomenon in question. To this end, we draw 
on primary sources, such as official Chinese government documents and newspaper 
articles, as well as secondary sources, including think tank reports and key academic 
literature. These materials are analysed through interpretative textual analysis, with-
out the application of formal coding procedures. Building on this, the study exam-
ines practical cases that empirically demonstrate the aforementioned role adaptation 
and analyses political narratives which, in line with the works of Shenhav (2006) 
and Miskimmon et al. (2013), are considered a political practice in their own right.
Additionally, and in order to gain a more nuanced understanding of the effects 
of the GSR on China’s international image, this analysis is complemented by semi-
structured interviews. In total, 5 interviews were conducted with experts from 
Europe, Central Asia, and Latin America. The interviewees were selected on the

provided written informed consent to participate in this study.
Some interviews were recorded with the permission of the interviewees, while 
others were conducted via e-mail. Thus, with the exception of Brazilian scholar Sab-
rina Medeiros, who opted for recorded interview, the rest of the experts preferred 
to answer questions previously sent by e-mail which consisted of the following four 
major questions: 1. How does Europe/Central Asia/Latin America 4 perceive Chi-

attempt to shape international perceptions on China?; 3. Which important projects 
would you like to highlight regarding the GSR in Central Asia/ Middle East/Latin 
America?; 4. Can the environmental commitment of an emerging power like China 
redefine global governance, especially in light of the hesitancy of the US to lead 
sustainable agendas?
The data obtained through the interviews were analysed using an interpretative 
textual analysis, and, for the sake of flexibility, no formal coding procedure was 
adopted.

forcing our overall analysis and findings.

4  The Greening of the BRI
After decades of significant pollution, China recognised that the traditional role con-
ception of progress at any cost to nature has proven disastrous. In a world increas-
ingly threatened by climate change, where both states and individuals demand more 
concrete measures for environmental protection, Beijing acknowledged that, in order 
to gain greater international influence and assume a future leadership role, it must 
adapt to contemporary demands and expectations. Consequently, the conception of 
China’s role has gradually shifted away from pollution-oriented growth towards Hu 
Jintao’s narrative of Scientific Development (China.org.cn 2012) and the ongoing 
Green BRI under Xi Jinping. This evolution in China’s understanding of nature and 
its role in protecting it has resulted in a role adaptation to align with both domestic 
and international expectations for a more responsible China in global environmental 
governance. In doing so, China seeks to reinforce and legitimise its status as a great 
power in all dimensions.
In this regard, the BRI can be considered an effective environmental pol-
icy instrument for China. The announcement of the GSR by Xi Jinping, in 2017, 
sparked an academic debate on why China decided to switch to the ‘greening’ of the 
BRI, thereby positioning itself as a ‘Green Great Power’ (Ferguson 2021; Khanal & 
Zhang 2024; Mardell 2023; Nitza-Makowska et al. 2024; Rodenbiker 2023). Thus, 
drawing on the current state of the art, we will now analyse the 3 primary reasons 
underlying the green shift of the BRI.
To begin with, China faces urgent domestic environmental degradation issues 
that require substantial efforts to mitigate their consequences. For example, accord-
ing to Swiss IQAir data from 2023, China ranks as the 19th country with the poor -
est air quality globally, with an Air Quality Index (AQI) of 94. In 2023, the average 
PM6 2.5 concentration in China’s air was 6.5 times above the World Health Organi-
sation’s recommended levels (IQAir 2023). Water pollution and scarcity have also 
emerged as critical issues in the country. Many rivers and lakes endure industrial 
discharge and agricultural runoff (Mardell 2023). Moreover, in the past 20  years, 
approximately 28,000 rivers have ceased to flow (Ferreira-Pereira et al. 2024; Tan & 
Lam 2022; K. Yan 2013). This situation is particularly significant for China, which 
has about 2,075 cubic metres of water per person. This figure is considerably below 
the global median of 3,776 cubic metres and roughly one-fifth of the per capita

Soil contamination by heavy metals and chemical pollutants has adversely 
affected agricultural productivity and food safety (Ashraf 2023). According to

China’s arable land is transitioning into desert due to factors such as water scarcity,

deteriorating environmental landscape in China, the GSR has become the so-called 
‘eco-certification’ of the Chinese government, as described by Yixian Sun (2022). 
This eco-certification requires the adoption of good practices for social, environ-
mental, and economic sustainability, both domestically and internationally, by speci-
fying rules for production (ibid.).
Secondly, according to Portuguese scholar António Queirós, 7 ‘the GSR fits well

Development.8 The Green BRI also empowers China to achieve its aim of carbon 
neutrality by 2060 (Xinhua 2023a).
Historically, these investments were centred on fossil fuel projects, sparking con-
cerns9 that the BRI participant states would adopt China’s high-emission develop-
ment model (Luthra & Gupta 2023). Nevertheless, during the UN General Assem-
bly, in September 2021, President Xi Jinping declared that China would cease 
investing in the construction of coal-fired power plants overseas (BBC 2021). Addi-
tionally, he promised to enhance assistance for green and low-carbon energy ini-
tiatives to other developing nations (ibid.). The Chinese President’s commitment 
appears in the Global Development Initiative Concept Paper 2021. This document 
highlights the BRI’s role in promoting sustainable development, particularly its 
environmental dimension (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of the People’s Republic of 
China 2021).
Finally, as the BRI was facing harsh criticism from the West as an environment-
unfriendly project, António Queirós stresses that ‘the GSR came to cope with the 
intensifying international concerns and to improve the global acceptance of the 
BRI’.10 The Green BRI aligns closely with global efforts to advance clean energy. 
Given China’s considerable potential to become the main clean energy power, the 
GSR strengthens Beijing’s capacity to assume a leadership role in global environ-
mental governance while simultaneously enhancing its international image.

Hence, the following section will focus on China’s renewable energy projects in 
three main regions: Central Asia, Latin America, and Europe. This analysis aims to 
reveal how China’s access to these regions’ clean energy infrastructure is perceived.
5  Chinese Renewable Energy Projects and its New Role Conception
Under the BRI, energy projects receive the largest share of investments. Yet, the 
Green Finance and Development Center’s official data show that China’s green 
energy investments have steadily risen, ultimately surpassing coal investments 
between 2017–2022 individually, however, they have not overtaken the overall 
investments in fossil fuels (see Table 1).
According to the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air Analysis, in 2023, 
the clean energy investment sector served as the sole contributor of the growth of 
China’s economic investments, while investments in other sectors declined. China 
invested approximately 6.3 trillion yuan ($890 billion) in clean energy sectors, an 
increase from 4.6 trillion yuan in 2022, marking a 1.7 trillion yuan (40 per cent) 
year-on-year rise (Myllyvirta 2024). Overall, clean energy accounted for 13 per cent 
of the total investment in fixed assets in China in 2023, an increase from nine per 
cent compared to the previous year (ibid.).
Moreover, the International Energy Agency (IEA) released an assessment of Chi-
na’s commitment to tripling global renewable energy capacity by 2030. The report 
highlighted that a 50 per cent surge in global renewable installations in 2023 resulted 
predominantly from China’s efforts. In 2022, China installed nearly as much solar 
photovoltaic capacity as the rest of the world combined (Cozzi et al. 2023). In 2023, 
the country doubled its new solar installations, increased new wind capacity by 66 
per cent, and nearly quadrupled the additions of energy storage (ibid.). Given the 
unmatched speed and scale of expansion in China’s installed renewable capacity,

Isabel Hilton (2024), the founder and former editor of China Dialogue, argues that

Starting with Central Asia, this region has always served as the primary focus 
of BRI investments due to its geographical and cultural proximity to China. In this 
context, China’s clean energy projects in Central Asia gained new relevance, par -
ticularly after the Russian invasion of Ukraine in 2022. However, Moscow increased 
the pressure on Central Asian countries to purchase more gas from Russia (Shar -
ifli 2024)). During our interview with Jildiz Nicharapova, 11 this scholar endorsed 
the view that China’s renewable energy projects in Central Asia play a crucial role 
in mitigating the region’s dependence on Russian gas and improving energy secu-
rity.12 In this regard, Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan are becoming major players in 
the region’s transition to renewable energy, particularly focusing on solar and wind 
power initiatives (Tanbaev 2024). As both countries plan to satisfy about 50 per cent 
of their energy consumption from renewable energy sources, Dr Nicharapova claims 
that China’s GSR interests the Central Asian republics.13
China’s investment in renewable energy in Central Asia is divided into two dis-
tinct phases. During the first phase (1990s–2018), alongside traditional energy ini-
tiatives, China primarily focused on large hydropower projects (HPPs) in the region. 
This effort particularly aided downstream countries, such as Kazakhstan and Uzbek-
istan, in exploiting their hydropower potential (Sharifli 2024). Chinese companies 
engaged in these projects mainly through debt financing from institutions such as 
the China Exim Bank (CEB) and the China Development Bank (CDB). From 2018 
onwards, China shifted its focus in the Central Asian renewable energy market from 
large HPPs to wind and solar projects (Zhou et al. 2022).
This transition included a change in financing strategies. Since 2018, Chinese 
renewable energy companies have shifted towards equity financing and increased 
collaboration with local development banks, replacing their earlier reliance on debt 
financing from Chinese banks (International Renewable Energy Agency 2023). This 
evolution in China’s corporate involvement aligns with the changing preferences of 
local governments in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, which are increasingly prioritis-
ing wind and solar projects due to the decreasing costs of these technologies com-
pared to large-scale hydropower plants (HPPs) and the negative impacts of climate 
change on hydro resources. However, Dr Nicharapova highlights the adverse impli-
cations of China’s involvement in Central Asia’s renewable energy infrastructure, 
suggesting that Russian fossil fuel dependency may be replaced by Chinese renew -
able energy sources (J. Nicharapova, personal communication, 24 May 2024).

China’s increasing solar exports present significant opportunities and challenges 
for Latin America. Affordable solar panels help the region reduce emissions, lower 
electricity costs, and enhance grid resilience, particularly during hot weather. Not-
withstanding, reliance on Chinese-made finished products may limit Latin Ameri-
ca’s ability to gain value within the solar supply chain. The risks are heightened if 
Chinese companies dominate the electricity sector, covering generation, transmis-
sion, and retail (Webster & Tobin 2024). China dominates Latin American electric-
ity markets as it is a key investor in the region’s generation, transmission, and dis-
tribution sectors. It maintains a near-monopoly on solar panels globally and plays a 
significant role in Latin America’s wind energy markets. Additionally, China seeks 
to secure a foothold in Argentina’s nuclear power plant industry (Kotz 2024).
China succeeded in penetrating the clean energy infrastructure of both developing 
and developed countries, with particular relevance to the EU. Following the Rus-
sian invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the EU aimed to reduce and eventually abolish 
its dependence on Russian fossil fuels by 2027, as outlined in the REPowerEU Plan 
2022. Currently, the EU Revised Renewable Energy Directive sets a target of 32 
per cent clean energy in the EU’s gross final energy consumption by 2030 (Euro-
pean Commission 2023). Surprisingly, China overtook the EU, which used to lead 
in renewable energy sources, back in 2018, causing losses for key clean energy play-
ers, including Denmark’s Vestas and German-owned Siemens Gamesa (POLITICO 
2024).
Chinese companies hold a substantial stake of around 10 per cent of the total 
installed wind energy capacity in the EU (Global Wind Energy Council 2024). More 
alarming for Brussels, China dominates the European solar panel market, supply -
ing around 80 per cent of the solar panels used in Europe (International Renewable 
Energy Agency 2022a). The EU has ‘acute reliance on China’ regarding renewa-
ble energy sources, making it unable to ‘go green without China’, at least for now, 
according to the European Union Institute for Security Studies (2024, p.2). This is 
corroborated by the EU’s former climate chief Wopke Hoekstra, who stated, ‘we are 
[the EU] getting more dependent on China in our quest to become greener, which is 
problematic’ (Simon 2024).
This evidence demonstrates China’s considerable efforts to invest in green pro-
jects across various regions, supporting our argument that China has been adapting 
its international role through the GSR. Indeed, China’s growing commitment to, and 
success in, green investment in regions such as Central Asia and Latin America have 
significant potential to enhance its international image both regionally and globally. 
By capitalising on structural needs and development gaps—particularly in green and 
sustainable development—China pursues a strategy that reflects an adaptation of its 
role performance in line with regional and international demands and expectations. 
In this way, China presents itself as a responsible power, attentive to regional needs 
and the future of humanity, thereby promoting a more attractive international image 
aligned with its interests. Through this strategy, Beijing seeks to secure recognition 
of its legitimacy to assume leadership in global environmental governance, while 
simultaneously strengthening its material capabilities to do so.

6  Results and Analysis of Survey Data
In order to help us understand whether China’s new role conception as a responsi-
ble stakeholder in environmental issues is, in fact, corroborated by the international 
community’s perceptions of the GSR’s performance, we will now discuss the main 
inputs resulting from the applied survey.
Regarding the question of how the GSR impacts China’s international image, the 
majority of respondents (64.8 per cent) indicated a ‘positive’ impact. Additionally, 
31.5 per cent reported that the GSR has a ‘moderate’ impact on China’s interna-
tional perception. Only 3.7 per cent believed that the GSR has ‘no impact’ on Chi-
na’s image, as illustrated in Fig. 1.
Regarding the inquiry on which specific direction of GSR projects respondents 
find most influential on China’s international perception, ‘Renewable Energy Pro-
jects’ received the highest favourability at 48.1 per cent. Meanwhile, ‘Green Infra-
structure Projects’ and ‘Innovative Green Technology Projects’ received 27.8 per 
cent and 20.4 per cent of the votes, respectively, as Fig. 2 indicates.
Finally, while addressing the primary motives for China’s shift to the ‘greening’ 
of the BRI, a larger share of respondents identified ‘China’s positioning as a Green 
Great Power’ as the key motive (46.3 per cent). Slightly fewer respondents (44.4 per 
cent) considered ‘coping with Western criticism of the BRI and improving its global 
acceptance’ as the main reason for the launch of the GSR. Additionally, 9.3 per cent 
of survey participants think that the domestic environmental degradation of China is 
responsible for Beijing’s green shift (Fig. 3).
Overall, the survey data indicates a strong alignment between China’s current 
role conception about environmental issues and the international community’s role 
expectations. The positive impact of the GSR on China’s international image, the 
significance of renewable energy projects, and the motives behind the greening of 
the BRI all suggest that China is perceived by our respondents as moving towards 
being a responsible stakeholder in environmental matters. Such a view is supported 
by Ana Barros-Platiau, 16 who contributes (or proposes/posits) that by positioning 
itself as a responsible environmental power, China demonstrates its willingness to 
occupy the spaces left vacant by the US, which could have implications for global 
governance.17 Nonetheless, the close percentage split between viewing China as 
positioning itself as a Green Great Power and coping with Western criticism sug-
gests that some scepticism remains about the authenticity of China’s environmental 
commitment. This dual perception portrays the complex interaction between genu-
ine (authentic or legitimate) environmental stewardship and strategic image man-
agement in China’s approach to global environmental issues. Such scepticism is 
also corroborated by Turkish scholar Berna Kirkulak-Uludag, 18 according to whom 
‘particularly developed countries are not convinced about [the GSR]’, while ‘less

7  Conclusion

of the GSR in improving the conception of China’s international role as an envi-
ronmental leader, this paper has attempted to answer the previously stated research 
question: To what extent does the GSR contribute to (re)adjust China’s role concep-
tion to better meet international expectations? By applying the role theory, we have 
arrived at the following conclusions.

As a first major finding, this study concludes that environmental issues are no 
longer indifferent to Beijing, both domestically and internationally. Although envi-
ronmental concerns were long neglected in favour of unrestrained progress, Chi-
na’s current role conception and role performance have adjusted in order to meet 
the expectations of the international community, which has been urging China to 
assume the role of a more responsible stakeholder in global environmental issues. 
In line with the tenets of Moral Realism, we also understand that by aligning itself 
with the expectations and demands of the international community, China seeks to 
present itself as a responsible, exemplary power In doing so, China can strengthen 
its international credibility, enhance its status, and thus, present itself as ready to fill 
the vacancies left by the United States.
When adopting its new role as a sustainable and environment-friendly power, 
China has three main motives underlying the ‘greening’ of the BRI: addressing 
domestic environmental degradation issues, positioning itself as a ‘Green Great 
Power’ by aligning with the UN Sustainable Development Agenda 2030, and coping 
with Western criticism to improve global acceptance of the BRI. In this role adapta-
tion, renewable energy projects receive most of the credit. China currently accounts 
for a quarter of the world’s solar energy capacity and one-third of the world’s wind 
power output. Given that approximately 50 per cent of the world’s energy could 
potentially come from solar and wind sources, China is poised to become the lead-
ing global superpower in clean energy. Yet, despite China’s push towards greening 
the BRI, the initiative’s dual commitment to both renewable and carbon-intensive 
energy sources present a complex scenario for future green energy development. An 
analytical approach reveals a tension between short-term economic gains from fos-
sil fuels and the long-term sustainability goals. As such, the environmental integrity 
of the BRI will depend on China’s ability to enforce stringent environmental regu-
lations across all BRI projects, potentially requiring a re-evaluation of its lending 
practices to favour sustainability over speed and cost. This shift could reduce the 
carbon footprint of the BRI, however it requires a recalibration of China’s energy 
strategy that prioritises long-term ecological health over short-term geopolitical and 
economic advantages.
Another important conclusion lies in the fact that China’s green initiatives are 
considered to be win–win for Central Asia and Latin America, because of these 
regions’ infrastructure deficit and energy transition problems. In contrast, China’s 
increasing involvement in the EU’s clean energy infrastructure is predominantly per-
ceived as a zero-sum game, because the EU used to be the leader in the renewable 
energy sector, while in 2018 China surprisingly overtook the EU’s leadership trig-
gering the competition. Possessing around 90 per cent of all installed wind and solar 
technologies in Latin America as well as supplying around 80 per cent of the solar 
panels used in Europe and approximately 30 per cent of solar and wind sources each 
in Central Asia, China’s role adjustment through GSR projects seems to be success-
ful. This fruitful role adjustment through the GSR projects links to our next finding.
Our survey confirms that the GSR, as an expression of significant role adapta-
tion, receives support from the majority of respondents, who believe the Initiative 
has positive impacts on China’s international image. Overall, China’s role perfor -
mance as a ‘Green Great Power’ largely meets external expectations for a more

responsible and proactive China in environmental matters. Nevertheless, our data 
also indicates the need for caution, as scepticism exists regarding the authenticity of 
China’s environmental commitment. This scepticism arises from the complex inter -
play between genuine environmental stewardship and strategic image management 
in China’s approach to global environmental issues. Besides, it should be stressed 
that despite China’s commendable initiatives to go green, the country remains the 
world’s biggest emitter of carbon dioxide. In other words, there is yet a considerable 
gap between the top green power that China would like to become and the current 
on-the-ground role performance. Undoubtedly, the GSR still has significant progress 
to make considering that it will most likely take several years, perhaps decades, to 
produce more impactful outcomes.
Given the significance of the topic and its ongoing developments, future studies 
should remain attentive to these dynamics. In particular, researchers should continue 
to monitor China’s green initiatives, assessing whether they will further strengthen 
China’s international image or, conversely, whether they may ultimately prove det-
rimental. In this regard, it would be worthwhile to examine these dynamics more 
closely by exploring the interplay between China’s green projects and the concept of 
greenwashing—a term, primarily used in economics and business that describes the 
practice of presenting positive environmental claims more as a marketing strategy 
than as a genuine commitment to sustainability. In any case, careful scrutiny of these 
dynamics will remain essential for understanding the international political agenda 
in environmental terms, as well as for shaping the future of geopolitics and global 
governance.
